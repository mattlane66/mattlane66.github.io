  1. 80/20 Rule

    The rule. Focus on the vital few that produce most of the results.

    When it helps. A small group of unresolved problems accounts for most of the remaining harm.

    Territory check. When the familiar top 20% is already well managed, the neglected 80% may contain more of the remaining opportunity.

    Signal. Returns from the usual priorities have flattened, while small neglected problems accumulate substantial harm.

    Better move. Recalculate where the next unit of effort creates the most value. Do not assume yesterday’s vital few are still vital.

    Question to settle. Where does the greatest remaining improvement per unit of effort actually lie?

  2. Inversion

    The rule. Find the ways to fail, then avoid them.

    When it helps. A preventable failure could cause an irreversible or unaffordable loss.

    Territory check. When failure is cheap and bounded but the upside of a few successful bets is enormous, avoiding mistakes can mean avoiding success.

    Signal. You can survive multiple failed experiments, but only a bold move has a chance to meaningfully change the outcome.

    Better move. Cap the downside and make calculated bets. Do not mistake zero errors for the best outcome.

    Question to settle. What can you afford to lose, and how much upside does caution forgo?

  3. Chesterton’s Fence

    The rule. Understand why something exists before you remove it.

    When it helps. An inherited practice protects against an important failure that people no longer notice.

    Territory check. Once the original function is understood and no longer exists, preserving the “fence” out of habit may do more harm than removing it.

    Signal. Nobody can identify a current purpose, and the practice creates measurable delay, cost, or harm.

    Better move. Remove or redesign the practice after checking for remaining dependencies. Understanding first does not mean preserving forever.

    Question to settle. What present-day function would actually be lost if this disappeared?

  4. Hanlon’s Razor

    The rule. Look for mistakes and misunderstandings before assuming bad intent.

    When it helps. A harmful handoff can be explained by unclear ownership or poor communication.

    Territory check. When someone repeatedly causes harm after clear feedback—and benefits from the result—a charitable story about intent can distract from the pattern.

    Signal. The behavior continues after expectations are explicit; the person faces little cost or gains convenience, status, or money.

    Better move. Set boundaries and consequences based on repeated behavior. You need not prove malicious intent to respond.

    Question to settle. What changed after feedback, and who benefits from the pattern?

  5. Parkinson’s Law

    The rule. Work expands to fill the time available; use constraints to prevent needless expansion.

    When it helps. The work is familiar, can be scoped cleanly, and is vulnerable to optional polishing.

    Territory check. When work is highly coupled, uncertain, or quality-critical, compressing the schedule can increase defects and rework rather than speed delivery.

    Signal. Unknown dependencies, unstable requirements, or quality that cannot be tested quickly.

    Better move. Timebox discovery, reduce uncertainty, and narrow the scope. An arbitrary deadline is not a substitute for understanding the work.

    Question to settle. Which parts can be cut without compromising the outcome?

  6. Regret Minimization

    The rule. Consider what your older self might regret not doing.

    When it helps. A valued opportunity may close, and fear of immediate discomfort is dominating the decision.

    Territory check. When the imagined preferences of a future self become a pretext for keeping every option open, the search for fewer regrets can create new ones.

    Signal. You repeatedly postpone commitments, accumulate open loops, and build little depth in relationships or skills.

    Better move. Commit to a direction worth deepening. Your future values may change; preserving optionality has a cost.

    Question to settle. Is this choice advancing a lasting value or merely postponing commitment?

  7. Pre-Mortem

    The rule. Imagine the plan has failed. Find and prevent likely causes.

    When it helps. The stakes are high and the consequences of failure are difficult to reverse.

    Territory check. When failures are cheap and feedback is fast, a long pre-mortem can cost more than a contained experiment.

    Signal. The team is debating remote edge cases while the core idea has never met real users.

    Better move. Put a limit on loss, ship a small test, and replace speculation with observed results.

    Question to settle. Would further prevention reduce more risk than the delay creates?

  8. Reversibility

    The rule. Use reversibility and the value of fresh information to decide when to commit.

    When it helps. New information is arriving soon, and waiting does not create dependencies or close the option.

    Territory check. A supposedly reversible decision can become sticky through contracts, habits, reputation, integration debt, or sunk costs. Waiting may make it harder to change.

    Signal. Switching costs grow with time; more people or systems are depending on the current path.

    Better move. Test or decide before lock-in compounds. Judge reversibility over time, not just on day one.

    Question to settle. How difficult will this be to reverse in three months, not merely today?

  9. Base Rates

    The rule. Start with what usually happens in comparable cases.

    When it helps. The new case has roughly the same constraints and causal drivers as past cases.

    Territory check. When a core driver of historical outcomes has genuinely changed, the old reference class can mislead rather than calibrate.

    Signal. A verified shift in cost, distribution, technology, or regulation removes a bottleneck present in the historical cases.

    Better move. Find or construct a better comparison and update the causal assumptions. Novelty alone is not evidence.

    Question to settle. Which historical drivers still hold, and which have demonstrably changed?

  10. Incentives

    The rule. Examine what people are rewarded for, formally and informally.

    When it helps. A team repeatedly sacrifices quality to meet a throughput target.

    Territory check. When loyalty, identity, craft, or principle dominates visible rewards, a narrow incentive-maximizing prediction can get behavior wrong.

    Signal. People repeatedly give up personal gain to protect quality, trust, belonging, or a commitment.

    Better move. Look beyond explicit rewards to values, identity, and norms. Do not let the model erase real motives.

    Question to settle. What choices do people make when rewards conflict with commitments?

  11. Goodhart’s Law

    The rule. When a measure becomes a target, optimizing it can erode its value as a proxy.

    When it helps. People can improve the reported number without improving what the number was meant to represent.

    Territory check. When a target closely measures the real outcome, resists gaming, and is independently checked, targeting it may not substantially weaken the measure.

    Signal. The target and an independently verified outcome keep improving together even after incentives change.

    Better move. Keep useful targets, with quality checks and ongoing validation. Do not assume every metric becomes useless when targeted.

    Question to settle. If everyone maximized this number, could the outcome we care about still get worse?
