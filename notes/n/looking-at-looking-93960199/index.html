Looking at Looking

The mind turns back to watch itself,

like a river starving for its current—

each glance creates another watcher,

infinite mirrors in a bare room.

I stand at the edge of thought,

marking the place where observation begins,

but the marker bleeds with every mark,

and the beginning keeps beginning again.

This is how wisdom comes:

not in the looking,

but in the raw space between looks,

where the mind forgets to watch

and simply flows, like water

finding its level.

I notice how my shadow stretches ahead,

then behind,

never quite where I am,

but always showing where I've been,

where I might go.

That's the trick of it:

to catch yourself not catching yourself,

to see the seeing when it isn't trying to be seen.

Like stars that burn brightest at the edge

of vision,

when you've almost stopped looking for them.

Discussion

I asked myself:

What does introspection outside of introspection look like?

It’s a fascinating philosophical question that invites us to examine the paradoxical nature of meta-cognition. When we try to observe the process of introspection from "outside," which we understand via science, we encounter something like a mental Möbius strip.

Consider a photographer trying to photograph themselves photographing - they might use mirrors or other cameras, but each attempt to capture the complete picture creates another layer of observation that itself isn't captured. Similarly, when we try to examine introspection from the outside, we're inevitably performing another act of introspection in the process.

However, we might approach this through different lenses:

From a phenomenological perspective, we could examine the traces or artifacts our introspective processes leave behind - like how meditation practitioners can observe changes in their thought patterns over time, not during meditation itself but in reflecting on its effects.

From a neuroscientific view, we might look at brain activity during introspective states, though this gives us correlates rather than the experience itself. It's like watching the ripples on water rather than seeing what caused them.
