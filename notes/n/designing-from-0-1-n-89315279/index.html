Designing From 0, 1, n

  1. Draw a boundary between the part of the world you plan to change and the part you will not change to isolate the forces acting within the "designable" focus area.
  2. Identify the forces that influence what you intend to design.
  3. Represent and resolve related forces in visualizations (e.g., sketches, state diagrams, etc.).
  4. Place the new design (form) into its living context and look for ***misfits ***(design problems) in the ensemble to improve fitness.

Forms: The objects of design.

Context: The system of forces (facts about the world that influence the form).

Fitness: The relationship between the form and the living context of the real world.

Requirements must not be attributes of the thing (form) being created; instead, they should describe facts about the world—things we cannot change. These requirements are generally technology-agnostic. This approach centers on designing with forces or patterns (i.e., recurring sets of forces).

Starting with these forces is a bottom-up design approach, i.e., focusing on the epicenter. These are the parts we compose from the inside out, the factors we must address to provide the capabilities to people in a specific situation with the best fit. To work this way, it is usually easiest to build with smaller chunks first and then aggregate them into larger wholes.

Discussion

Why Function ≠ Ensemble

  1. Function is Local; Ensemble is Global

    • A function is specific to a form—it describes what the form does and how it behaves.
    • The ensemble is the broader system—it includes the form, the forces acting upon it, and the relationships between all elements.

  2. Function is About Role; Ensemble is About Composition

    • Function describes how a form serves a purpose within a system.
    • The ensemble describes how multiple forms and forces interrelate as a whole.

  3. Fitness Comes from the Interaction Between Function and Ensemble

    • If a function does not fit well within the ensemble, misfits arise.
    • Adjusting function (or form) improves fitness in relation to the ensemble.
      You can get some guide to what the function of something is by looking at “charecteristic use”.

Example: The Shared Family Calendar App

Draw a Boundary

  1. Designable Focus Area

    • You’re tasked with improving how a family (household members) manages and shares events, tasks, and schedules digitally.
    • It might be a mobile app or web app—some digital “calendar” system that’s accessible to all family members.

  2. Out of Scope

    • You cannot change people’s workplaces or school systems (i.e., you can’t force a school to adopt your calendar).
    • You also can’t alter major cloud platforms (e.g., Google/Apple) if you’re merely integrating with them.
    • You can’t change broad cultural aspects of how families communicate in general.
      By drawing this boundary, you isolate the realm you can truly influence—the family’s shared tool—and acknowledge broader forces you cannot directly change.

Identify the Forces

Think of context as the dynamic web of facts and conditions around family life:

  1. Social & Emotional Forces

    • Parent–Child Dynamics: Parents need oversight of kids’ schedules but also want kids to learn responsibility.
    • Family Bonding: Some families see scheduling as a reflection of quality time or personal values (e.g., scheduling “family dinner”, and balancing chores).

  2. Functional Forces

    • Reminder & Notification Overload: People want timely notifications but hate being spammed.
    • Offline vs. Online Needs: Some family members (e.g., grandparents) may prefer minimal tech or might not have smartphones.

  3. Practical Constraints

    • Varied Tech Savviness: One sibling might be a tech wizard, and another only checks email occasionally.
    • Different Device Ecosystems: Some on iOS, some on Android, others on a shared home computer.
    • Time Zones: If any family members travel often or live in different locations, time zone differences can confuse scheduling.
      Requirements here are not “the app must have a purple UI;” they’re facts about the world—such as “Grandma uses a feature phone” or “Kids forget to check their notifications.” You can’t change these truths; you have to accommodate them.

Represent & Visualize the Forces

You might sketch a quick diagram:

  • X-axis: Level of Technological Comfort (low → high)
  • Y-axis: Need for Coordination (few shared events → many shared events)
    Mark different family members on this chart. This helps you see potential friction points—e.g., the ones with low tech comfort but high coordination needs will be a challenge.

Alternatively, you could outline a state diagram of how events get created, shared, and updated, and highlight where conflicts or errors occur.

Put the Current Form (Point A) Into Context & Identify Misfits

Current Approach might be:

  • A “patchwork” of solutions:

    • Mom uses Google Calendar, Dad uses Apple Calendar, kids rely on text messages, and Grandma calls on the phone.
      Common Misfits You Might Observe:
  1. Schedule Overlaps: No single shared view leads to double bookings (Dad books a weekend trip while Mom schedules a family reunion).
  2. Outdated Information: Some events get updated in one tool but never cascade to others.
  3. Uneven Engagement: Teenagers ignore texts; grandparents never see app notifications.
  4. Frustration & Blame: Family members blame each other for “not paying attention” or “not sending the invite.”
    These misfits—where friction is highest—point to areas needing the most urgent design attention.

Uncover Needs & Insights (Latent Forces)

By interviewing family members, you discover deeper motivations:

  • Latent Need 1: “I want to keep track of everyone’s activities without feeling like a nag.”

    • Insight: Constant reminders or check-ins can feel controlling; a gentle, automated way to update schedules could reduce tension.

  • Latent Need 2: “I want our calendar to reflect fun or personal moments, not just chores and appointments.”

    • Insight: A purely functional calendar might miss the emotional aspect of family life—like highlighting birthdays, shared photos, or small wins (e.g., a child’s school achievement).

  • Latent Need 3: “I’m worried I’ll miss something important if I don’t see the notification right away.”

    • Insight: People may need a fallback or summary view (e.g., a daily text summary or a quick phone call to older relatives).
      These latent needs go beyond the obvious “We just need one shared Google Calendar.” They reveal emotional, social, and practical forces a solution must address.

Generate Forms (Explore Radically Different Ideas)

Explore multiple solutions that might meet these forces:

  1. Unified Family Hub

    • A web and mobile app that automatically syncs with Google, Apple, and Outlook calendars in real time.
    • Each family member sees a single aggregated “family feed” of events.
    • Allows quick color coding: Red for urgent tasks, Green for fun events, etc.

  2. Low-Tech Notification Bridge

    • In addition to a central app, an automated SMS or phone-call system for members without smartphones (“Grandma Hotline”).
    • A daily or weekly “digest” call that reads out the upcoming events.

  3. Gamified Calendar

    • Points or stickers for chores or tasks completed, so kids engage.
    • Family can unlock “rewards” (movie night, pizza coupons) when everyone stays on track for a week.

  4. Storyboard Calendar

    • A digital corkboard + calendar hybrid: You can attach images, notes, or voice clips for events.
    • A timeline that feels more like a social feed, capturing special moments as well as appointments.
      Each idea addresses different misfits—some focus on bridging tech-savvy gaps, others emphasize emotional warmth and fun, and others tackle real-time synchronization.

Stage the Ensemble: Prototype & Test

Prototyping means you build light, testable versions to see how they fit into real family routines:

  1. Paper or Clickable Mockups

    • Create a simple interactive prototype (using tools like Figma or pen-and-paper storyboards) that demonstrates how events are posted, updated, and viewed.

  2. Role-Play with Family Scenarios

    • Write short scripts—Mom needs to schedule a doctor’s appointment, Dad sets a meeting at the same time, the child sees a clash, etc.
    • Let participants “act it out” using the mockup, noticing where confusion arises.

  3. Early Beta Testing

    • Implement minimal functionality: A basic web app to share events with an SMS reminder option for offline members.
    • Have a real family try it out for a week and gather feedback on friction points.
      Misfits discovered in testing might include:
  • Overwhelming color-coding: “I’m color-blind; all these categories look the same.”
  • Confusing reminders: “I got 5 notifications for the same event.”
  • Privacy concerns: “I don’t want extended family to see my medical appointments.”
    These issues are exactly what you want to reveal in staging the ensemble (the interplay of your prototype with the real context).

Refine Misfits & Iterate

Address the most critical or “obtrusive” misfits:

  • Too Many Notifications → Add a weekly digest or flexible notification settings.
  • Privacy → Allow event-level privacy controls or “public” vs “private” designations.
  • Visual Accessibility → Offer high-contrast themes and bigger fonts.
    You continuously adjust form (the app) to align it better with context (the diverse family needs). Each iteration moves you closer to a solution that “just works” in daily life.

Achieve a Good Fit

Ultimately, after iterative testing and refinement, you converge on a Shared Family Calendar App that:

  1. Syncs Seamlessly with each family member’s existing system (Google, Apple, etc.).
  2. Offers SMS Summaries or simple phone-call reminders for those less comfortable with smartphone apps.
  3. Balances Fun & Utility by allowing short notes, photos, or emojis so the calendar reflects family life beyond deadlines.
  4. Respects Privacy with straightforward event-sharing controls.
    Because you staged and tested your prototypes in realistic family scenarios, you systematically rooted out misfits. The final ensemble—form plus context—feels natural and frictionless, creating a genuine sense of good fit.

Key Takeaways

  1. Context First: Real design requirements come from unchangeable facts about the family (ages, tech comfort, daily routines).
  2. Misfits as Guides: Let friction points (duplicated appointments, privacy concerns) guide you to where the solution should evolve.
  3. Multiple Form Explorations: Don’t settle on “just an app”; test radically different angles (gamification, bridging offline/online, adding emotional warmth).
  4. Staging the Ensemble: Actively prototype and observe how the “family plus calendar app” interacts in the real world.
  5. Iterate for Best Fit: Each cycle of testing and refining edges you closer to a solution that fits seamlessly into users’ lives.
    This Shared Family Calendar App example demonstrates how to design from the inside out—starting with the unchangeable forces (context) and systematically creating forms that eliminate misfits and deliver real value to everyday users.

Designing From 0, 1, n

Key Idea: Draw a boundary around the part of the world you will change (form) and the part you won’t (context). Identify forces in the context, place the new form into that environment, and refine any “misfits.”

  • Decomposition:

    • Separates form (the designed solution) from context (constraints, user environment, social/emotional factors).
    • Identifies sub-forces or specific constraints (e.g., cost limits, user emotional states).

  • Abstraction:

    • Treats the environment as a force field—intangible but impactful constraints that guide the form.
    • “Requirements” become facts about the world that you can’t change; you design the form around these forces.

  • Pattern Recognition:

    • You repeatedly test form against context, noticing misfits or friction that emerges in the ensemble.
    • Over multiple designs, certain recurring friction points become predictable or repeated.

  • Algorithm/Procedure:

    • Follows a refinement loop: place the form into the context → observe friction → adjust form → retest.
    • Continues until you reach a “good fit” or run out of major misfits to address.
