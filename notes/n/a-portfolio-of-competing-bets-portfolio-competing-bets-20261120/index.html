A Portfolio of Competing Bets

How to invest in an emerging market when the winning business model is uncertain.

An emerging market can develop in several ways. Different companies may serve different customers, solve different problems, and capture value through different business models.

Each represents a bet on how the market will develop.

The investment problem is to understand which strategies can produce superior businesses, which businesses are worth owning, and whether combining their investments improves the portfolio.

1. Map the competing strategies

Start with a Blue Ocean Strategy canvas.

Map competing offerings against the factors customers value. Group those factors by the parts of the larger system they serve.

Consider longitudinal healthcare. One company might control the patient relationship. Another might become indispensable to clinical workflows. Others might help insurers reduce costs or pharmaceutical companies produce research evidence.

Each occupies a different strategic position. Each depends on different conditions for success.

The canvas makes those differences visible. It does not establish which strategy will be profitable.

That requires understanding the business mechanics.

2. Connect product performance to business economics

For each strategy, identify the two leading product metrics most likely to influence its economic performance.

A patient-first healthcare business might depend on:

  • Usable, permissioned patient records: The number of records sufficiently complete and authorized for a valuable use.
  • Patient retention: The proportion of participants who continue contributing useful information.

These measures could determine the quality and availability of longitudinal evidence. But they create revenue only if someone will pay for that evidence.

The business model connects product performance to paying customers, revenue, direct costs, and recurring gross profit.

That connection must be demonstrated. Improving a product metric does not necessarily improve the business.

The same reasoning applies to competitive advantage. A differentiated offering becomes economically superior when its differences improve willingness to pay, reduce costs, or create advantages competitors cannot easily neutralize.

Recurring gross profit is an important outcome, but not a complete measure of business value. Growth, operating expenses, reinvestment, capital requirements, and durability also matter.

3. Underwrite the investment, not just the business

A superior business is not necessarily a superior investment.

Investment returns depend on the price paid, ownership acquired, subsequent dilution, additional capital required, exit outcomes, and time.

Two measures make those consequences explicit:

  • MOIC: How much capital the investment returns relative to the amount invested.
  • IRR: The annualized return, accounting for the timing of investment cash flows.

Both should be evaluated across plausible outcomes, including failure and exceptional success. Those scenarios also require estimates of their likelihood, with uncertainty made explicit.

A company might become the market leader and still disappoint its investors if they paid too much or retained too little ownership.

The objective is not simply to identify winners. It is to acquire attractive claims on their possible future value.

4. Understand how the bets depend on one another

Different competitive strategies are not necessarily independent investments.

A patient-first company and a provider-first company might have different customers, products, and revenue models. Yet both could depend on access to reliable, legally usable longitudinal patient information.

If that condition fails, both investments could suffer.

Other conditions might benefit one strategy while undermining another. Some companies may compete for the same economic value; others may complement one another.

Mapping these relationships reveals which assumptions the portfolio depends on repeatedly.

Shared dependencies are evidence of potential common risks, not proof of statistical correlation. Actual diversification benefits depend on the joint distribution of investment outcomes.

Under suitable assumptions, combining individually attractive investments with imperfectly correlated returns can reduce portfolio variance without reducing expected return.

But diversification is not the objective by itself.

The objective is exceptional investment returns at an acceptable level of risk. A new investment must justify both the capital it consumes and its contribution to the portfolio. Owning direct competitors may also introduce conflicts of interest and confidentiality obligations.

5. Learn before committing more than necessary

Donald Reinertsen's The Principles of Product Development Flow provides a useful discipline for making these decisions.

Several principles are particularly relevant:

  • Quantified overall economics and interconnected variables (E1, E2): Judge changes by their effects on the whole economic system, not isolated metrics.
  • Maximum economic influence and leading indicators (FF1, FF3): Focus measurement on variables that meaningfully influence economic results.
  • Fast learning and buying information (FF8, E18): Obtain evidence when its expected decision value justifies its cost.
  • Optimum decision timing (E15): Balance the benefit of learning more against the cost of waiting.
  • Negative covariance (V10): Examine whether different exposures can counterbalance variability.

Applying these product development principles to venture investing is a proposed extension, not a proven investment formula.

For each investment, identify the next decisive learning.

What assumption most affects its prospective value? What evidence would change the investment decision? How quickly and economically can that evidence be obtained?

This is especially important in the age of AI.

AI can reduce the time and capital required to build products and test alternatives. Investors can therefore expect founders to demonstrate more with fewer resources.

But faster implementation is not necessarily faster learning. Customer adoption, willingness to pay, retention, and durable economic performance still require credible observation.

The founder's advantage increasingly depends on the ability to choose what matters, design informative tests, interpret evidence, and adapt appropriately.

The aim is not maximum experimentation. It is maximum economically useful learning.

6. Allocate capital as the evidence changes

The model brings these ideas together:

Competitive strategy → Product metrics → Business economics → Investment returns → Portfolio contribution

Learning connects every stage.

New evidence may change which strategy appears attractive, which business is most likely to execute it, what ownership is worth, or whether another investment adds useful diversification.

Each new capital decision should reflect those changes.

Sometimes the right decision is to invest early, before the opportunity is widely recognized. Sometimes it is to investigate further, wait, decline, or commit additional capital to an existing investment.

The question is not merely which company might win.

It is which available investment offers the best prospective use of capital, given what we know, what remains uncertain, and what we already own.

The model is a method for making those judgments explicit, testing them, and revising them as evidence arrives. Whether it improves realized venture returns remains an empirical question.

Find different ways to win. Establish their economics. Learn what matters. Own the opportunities worth owning without repeatedly betting on the same way to lose.
