Design What Belongs

Design is the work of deciding what belongs, why it belongs, how the parts work together, and whether the result makes a difference.

The principles below connect those judgments. They are not steps that must happen in a fixed order. New evidence can change an earlier decision.

Understand — what matters?

Start with the outcome. Define the change you want to make, not a list of features or tasks.

Observe real behavior. Study what people do, why they do it, and where their current approach fails. A request is evidence to examine, not automatically a requirement.

State requirements before mechanisms. Describe what a solution must achieve or preserve. Leave room for different ways to satisfy those conditions.

Choose — what is worth pursuing?

Compare different approaches. Contrast reveals assumptions, tradeoffs, and possibilities that one idea alone can hide.

Check the fit. Compare candidate solutions against the same requirements. Record what each satisfies, what it fails, and what remains uncertain.

Bound the investment. Decide how much the opportunity is worth. Resolve consequential unknowns before committing too much, then shape the scope to fit.

Compose — what belongs together?

Find the load-bearing decision. Resolve the part of the design on which other choices depend before building around it.

Reverse the Fit Check. For each part of the chosen solution, ask which requirement it serves. Remove or revise parts that have no clear reason to exist.

Design the relationships. Show how the parts connect, interact, and affect one another. A better relationship may matter more than a better part.

Set boundaries by dependency. Separate parts that can change independently. Keep together what must work together.

Preserve conceptual integrity. Use consistent concepts and rules. Each part should support a coherent whole rather than add an exception without a good reason.

Protect — what must remain true?

Record design invariants. State the essential behavior, relationships, and constraints that a change must preserve.

Bound authority. Make clear what people and agents may change, which decisions require approval, and how consequential changes will be checked.

Verify — does it work in reality?

Test form against context. A mechanism can work as designed and still fail in the conditions where people use it.

Match interaction to judgment. Let people describe what is easy to say, scan what is easier to recognize, and inspect what they need to judge.

Use friction deliberately. Make useful actions easy. Add friction when it helps prevent mistakes or harmful actions.

Separate conformance from effect. Test whether the product meets its requirements. Separately test whether it produces the intended change.

Evolve — does the next change make the system stronger?

Let evidence revise the design. When results differ from expectations, examine the requirements, assumptions, structure, and implementation. Do not protect a mistaken model.

Make improvements compound. Reuse useful knowledge and capabilities. Favor changes that make future improvements easier without adding unnecessary complexity.

Design reinforcing incentives. Where possible, make adoption, participation, and competitors' rational choices strengthen the system. Do not depend on others making mistakes.

Three relationships to keep visible

Requirements ↔ Solution. Does the solution satisfy what matters, and does every part have a reason to exist?

Design ↔ Implementation. Does each change preserve the essential structure, behavior, and constraints?

Product ↔ Reality. Does the product produce the intended outcome, and what must change when it does not?

Together, they place three demands on a design: fitness with its purpose and context; integrity among its parts; and generativity—the ability to preserve or expand valuable future possibilities.

Design for what must be true. Make each part serve that purpose. Preserve what works, revise what does not, and let each improvement strengthen the whole.
