Opportunity Leveling

In a just society, people can reasonably expect the benefits attached to their roles under fair rules. But it is a mistake to think we inherently deserve a society that highly rewards the talents or traits we happen to possess. Inequalities are justified only when they make the least advantaged better off than they would be under a more equal arrangement.

**Simple Difference Principle Examples**

**Paying doctors more:** If higher pay is needed to attract and keep talented doctors, and that leads to better care, shorter wait times, or more clinics in poor and rural areas, then the inequality can be justified. The key is that the least advantaged are better off than they would be under a more equal system. If higher pay makes doctors richer without improving access to or care for those who are worse off, then the inequality is not justified.

**CEO compensation:** If higher CEO pay is part of a system that grows firms in ways that raise wages, improve training, and expand opportunity for the lowest-paid workers, then the inequality may be justified. But if executives receive large bonuses while entry-level workers see stagnant pay, weaker benefits, or less security, then the inequality fails the test.

**Higher pay for teachers in poor districts:** If paying teachers more helps attract strong teachers to underserved schools and improves outcomes for poor students, the inequality can be justified. If the extra pay mainly benefits already advantaged schools or does not materially improve the condition of the least advantaged students, then it is not justified.

This is the core idea: inequality is not justified because the talented deserve more, but because it must work to the benefit of those worst off.
