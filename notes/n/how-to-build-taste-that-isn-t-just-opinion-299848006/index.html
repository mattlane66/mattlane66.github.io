How to Build Taste That Isn’t Just Opinion

Taste is judgment, not objective proof. But developed taste is more than preference: it is perceptive, informed, intentional, comparative, context-sensitive, and accountable to consequences.

  1. Separate Mechanics from Aesthetics

“Competence is the price of admission for preference.” Fix execution errors before debating artistic choices. Sloppiness is not a style.

  1. Test for Intentionality

“Luck stumbles into brilliance; taste builds a path back to it.” If you cannot recreate the result deliberately, you may have been lucky rather than skillful.

  1. Isolate the Variables

“If you can’t name the lever, you don’t control the output.” Identify what is producing the effect before deciding whether it works.

  1. Understand the Standard Before Departing

“Deviating by design is taste; deviating by accident is ignorance.” Know what prevailing conventions accomplish, where they fail, and why you are breaking them.

  1. Sharpen Perception

“You can only judge what you can perceive.” Taste depends on noticing distinctions in proportion, pacing, texture, balance, contrast, rhythm, and noise.

  1. Judge Within Context

“An aesthetic choice without context is directionless.” The same choice may feel obvious, radical, dated, or premature depending on its purpose, audience, culture, and moment.

  1. Practice Restraint

“Elegance is resisting the urge to add.” Every element should justify the attention, complexity, and friction it introduces.

  1. Preserve Productive Tension

“Structure builds the form; friction gives it a soul.” Perfect harmony can become sterile. Contrast, asymmetry, ambiguity, and controlled imperfection can make the whole more powerful.

  1. Compare, Don’t Merely Consume

“Taste grows through comparison, not consumption.” Study strong, weak, conventional, and unusual examples. Ask what changed, what each gains or loses, and why one succeeds where another fails.

  1. Calibrate Through Consequences

“Taste improves when judgment meets reality.” Observe what the work actually makes people notice, understand, feel, remember, and do. Recalibrate when the intended effect does not survive outside your own head.

  1. Establish a Standard of Value

“Without a hierarchy of values, taste becomes sophisticated inconsistency.” Clarity, beauty, originality, usefulness, accessibility, and emotional force often conflict. Taste means deciding which values govern this work and making tradeoffs accordingly.

Taste develops through a continuous cycle:

Exposure → Perception → Description → Standards → Intention → Execution → Comparison → Consequences → Recalibration

The goal is not to eliminate subjectivity. It is to turn unexamined preference into disciplined judgment.
