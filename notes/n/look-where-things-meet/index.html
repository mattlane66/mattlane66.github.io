Look Where Things Meet

A traditional espresso machine takes prepared grounds in a filter basket inside its portafilter. A Nespresso capsule holds a measured dose of coffee, but doesn't fit that brewing arrangement. How could one machine offer both ways of making coffee?

Start with the work. You grind, dose, and tamp because the machine expects coffee prepared in a particular form. Trace those steps backward and you reach the filter basket inside the portafilter, where the coffee must fit.

Or start with a possibility. The capsule already measures and contains the coffee. Could that capability serve someone using a traditional machine? If so, where would the capsule need to meet the machine?

Compare where to intervene. The pump already supplies pressurized water. The capsule already provides convenience. Replacing the whole machine would change much more than necessary. The basket is removable and sits at the meeting point. A replacement might accept the capsule while leaving those other functions intact.

The basket is promising because it controls a consequential requirement, can be replaced, and leaves much of the existing system alone. This is interface adaptation: a physical adapter changes how otherwise incompatible parts meet, without redesigning the machine or capsule. If it works, one machine can offer traditional preparation or capsule convenience.

The point isn't to find where things merely touch. It's to discover which relationship limits—or could expand—what people can do.

How to find it elsewhere

  1. Start with the desired experience. What should someone be able to do, and under what conditions?
  2. Look for work or possibility. What forces people to prepare, translate, wait, or coordinate? What existing capabilities could be combined in a new way?
  3. Find the governing requirement. What interface, component, handoff, or rule causes the work—or keeps the capabilities apart?
  4. Compare interventions. What must remain true? Where could an existing capability meet the requirement differently? Which change has the greatest effect with the least disruption?
  5. Test the whole experience. What new choices appear? Does the change fit the real conditions, or does the difficulty move somewhere else?

Try it today

Notice someone working around an awkward connection. Or notice two existing capabilities that might be useful together. Find the requirement that governs the relationship, compare where you could change it, and imagine the resulting experience.

The opportunity may not be to improve either thing. It may be to change what they can do together.

Language for the ideas Optional · 15 terms
Interface
How two things meet or exchange something—materials, information, decisions, or actions.
Boundary
Where one part, role, or responsibility ends and another begins.
Dependency
Something that must happen or hold true before another thing can proceed.
Coupling
How strongly two things affect or constrain one another. Sometimes coordination matters more than independence.
Constraint
A condition a design must accommodate. Some are essential; others come from choices or conventions.
Interface adaptation
Changing how existing things meet so otherwise incompatible parts can work together.
Adapter pattern (physical or software)
A way to reconcile incompatible interfaces. A formal software pattern, with physical counterparts such as this basket.
Interoperability
The ability of separately designed things to work together.
Retrofit design
Adding a capability to an existing product or system instead of replacing the whole thing.
Design for compatibility
Designing something to work with existing products, formats, or systems.
Fit / misfit
How well an arrangement serves its real conditions—or where requirements conflict with it.
Modularity / orthogonality
Keeping parts independent where possible, while designing together what must work together.
Leverage point
A place where changing a relationship, rule, or flow can have wider consequences.
Control factor
A condition or design variable you can choose or change in this effort.
Selective coupling
Make the parts that must cooperate work together; leave unrelated parts independent.
