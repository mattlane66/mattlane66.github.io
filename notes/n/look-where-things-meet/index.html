Look Where Things Meet

A traditional espresso machine takes prepared grounds in a filter basket inside its portafilter. A Nespresso capsule holds a measured dose of coffee, but doesn't fit that brewing arrangement. How could one machine offer both ways of making coffee?

Follow the work backward. You grind, dose, and tamp because the machine expects coffee in a particular form. The basket is where that requirement becomes physical. It is worth examining—but it isn't yet the answer.

Compare where you could intervene. The pump already provides pressurized water. The capsule already contains the coffee. Replacing the whole machine would sacrifice something that works. The basket, however, is removable and sits where the coffee meets the brewing system. A replacement might accommodate the capsule without changing the machine or the capsule.

That is the leverage: a small change at a consequential interface might preserve the working parts while changing the preparation required. The same machine can offer traditional control or the convenience of a capsule.

The point isn't to find where things merely touch. It's to find where an arrangement enforces a requirement—and where changing it could create a better experience.

How to find it elsewhere

  1. Start with the desired experience. What should someone be able to do, and under what conditions?
  2. Trace the work backward. Where must people prepare, translate, wait, coordinate, or follow a rule? What makes that necessary?
  3. Find where the requirement is enforced. Which interface, component, handoff, or rule determines what is possible?
  4. Compare possible changes. What must stay true? What existing capability could satisfy the requirement differently? What could connect, separate, adapt, or reorder without disturbing what works?
  5. Test the whole experience. What new choices appear? Does the change fit the real conditions, or does the difficulty move somewhere else?

Try it today

Notice someone doing work to make things work together—or obeying a rule nobody has questioned. Trace the work to the requirement. Compare where you could intervene, then imagine what becomes possible.

The opportunity may not be to improve either thing. It may be to change what they can do together.

Language for the ideas Optional · 12 terms
Interface
How two things meet or exchange something—materials, information, decisions, or actions.
Boundary
Where one part, role, or responsibility ends and another begins.
Dependency
Something that must happen or hold true before another thing can proceed.
Coupling
How strongly two things affect or constrain one another. Sometimes coordination matters more than independence.
Constraint
A condition a design must accommodate. Some are essential; others come from choices or conventions.
Interoperability
The ability of separately designed things to work together.
Adapter
A component or process that reconciles different interfaces without redesigning everything involved.
Fit / misfit
How well an arrangement serves its real conditions—or where requirements conflict with it.
Modularity / orthogonality
Keeping parts independent where possible, while designing together what must work together.
Leverage point
A place where changing a relationship, rule, or flow can have wider consequences.
Control factor
A condition or design variable you can choose or change in this effort.
Selective coupling
Make the parts that must cooperate work together; leave unrelated parts independent.
