How Framing (Demand Side) and Shaping (Supply Side) Ping‐Pong Plays Out

Sometimes you have a problem and no solution.

Other times you start with a solution, but you haven't yet worked backward to the problem.

The reality is that it’s not a linear process but a moving back-and-forth motion between problem (demand) and solution (supply).

Example Scenario

Let’s imagine you have the germ of an idea for a new product that automatically **records and transcribes customer calls **for busy sales teams.

Demand Side (Problem)

“We always forget important client details from our calls, and it’s awkward typing notes during the call. We want an automatic way to capture calls and quickly find what was said.”

  1. Initial Observation:

    • Sales reps complain they can’t fully focus on the conversation because they’re juggling note‐taking.
    • Managers suspect many follow‐up tasks get dropped because nobody can remember all the action items.

  2. Research/Questions:

    • “Are reps already recording calls in some way? If so, do they listen back?”
    • “How many calls do they have per day? 3–4 or 20+?”
    • “Would they want full‐length recordings or just a summary of key points?”

  3. Finding:

    • 80% of calls are <15 minutes.
    • The biggest pain is that reps don’t want to sift through a 15‐minute replay for one detail. They just want quick highlights or text search.
      Supply Side (Solution Directions)

“We could build a call‐recording and transcription service baked into our existing chat app or as a stand‐alone product.”

  1. Initial Idea:

    • Idea: Integrate call recording + AI transcription so reps can replay or read a transcript anytime.
    • Constraint: Building an in‐house transcription engine is huge. Might need a third‐party transcription API.

  2. Prototyping & Feasibility:

    • “Let’s do a small experiment with an off‐the‐shelf speech‐to‐text API to see if the accuracy is good enough.”
    • Discovery: The API costs $X per minute. For high volume, that might get expensive.

  3. Refinement:

    • “Because the main pain is finding what was said, we don’t need an ultra‐fancy audio interface. A robust text search might do 90% of the job.”
    • “We can keep actual audio recordings for a limited time (e.g. 30 days) to save storage costs.”
      Reflects a Messy Reality, and That’s Ok

  4. Starting with a Fuzzy Problem: You begin on the demand side: “Our sales teams can’t keep track of key info from calls.”

  5. Jumping to a Possible Solution: Next, you hypothesize: “What if we record and transcribe calls automatically?”

  6. Going Back to Validate the Problem: Before building anything, you go back to ask real users: “Do you truly want whole‐call playback, or is searching transcripts enough?”

  7. Prototyping to Explore Feasibility (Supply): You try an off‐the‐shelf transcription API to see how accurate it is and weigh costs.

  8. Refining the Scope: The new knowledge (costs, user desire for quick search) shapes a narrower solution: you prioritize text search as the key feature, with optional audio access for a limited time.
    Throughout, each demand‐side question sets up a new supply‐side prototype or research, and each supply‐side insight triggers further questions on the demand side. This is exactly the iterative shaping process: you **bounce back and forth **until the scope and solution match the real need.

The back and forth between the supply side and demand side defined a context that made it clear at each step what the question was, why I was asking it, and how the answer affected the work-in-progress solution.

This also shows Kidlin's Law in action. That the problem and the solution are much closer in kind than we think.

Discussion

Framing (Demand) ↔ Shaping (Supply) Ping-Pong

Key Idea: An iterative, back-and-forth process of clarifying the problem context (demand side) and refining potential solution directions (supply side).

  • Decomposition:

    • Splits the design space into two perspectives: Problem (user context, pain points) and Solution (prototypes, technology feasibility).
    • Each side can be investigated in smaller, more focused steps (user research, tech spikes).

  • Abstraction:

    • Remains open-ended at first, focusing on big-picture questions: “What do people really need?” vs. “Which solution directions might address this?”
    • Avoids locking into a single detailed form too soon.

  • Pattern Recognition:

    • Each iteration reveals recurring friction points or consistent user demands.
    • Repeatedly seeing the same issues helps you refine or discard solution paths more efficiently.

  • Algorithm/Procedure:

    • Involves a loop: (1) Clarify the problem → (2) Suggest solution → (3) Validate → (4) Refine → return to (1).
