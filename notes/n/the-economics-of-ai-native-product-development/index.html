The economics of AI-native product development.

When software becomes cheaper to produce, the advantage moves toward deciding what is worth making—and how best to make it.

More generated software is not necessarily more value. Human judgment, verification, integration, and failure remain costly. Two decisions help direct investment: choose a bet on the evidence, then choose the least costly acceptable way to execute it.

The successful outcome's benefit is reduced by two weeks of delay only if built after the test. The test itself costs money whether or not you build. Benefit and costs are expressed in the same economic units; user value still needs independent real-world evidence.
Appetite is a separate judgment.First decide whether the opportunity deserves investment. Then choose the maximum amount of human time, machine execution, money, and calendar time you are prepared to commit—and the quality floor that must not move. An estimated build cost is not that commitment.

02 / Cost of execution

What is the least costly acceptable route?

The cheapest production is not always the cheapest acceptable result.

Once a bet is worth making, allocate its tasks within the appetite and quality floor. For one representative task, compare a direct agent, an agent with human review, a specialist, and a deterministic system. Exclude routes that cannot meet the stipulated constraints before comparing total expected cost.

Expected total = production + verification + delay + escaped-failure probability × loss. Here failure loss includes expected rework/recovery. All route base costs and durations are hypothetical: direct agent $24 / 0.1 day; reviewed agent $24 + selected review cost / 0.65 day; specialist $260 / 2 days; deterministic system $140 / 0.6 day. Specialist failure is assumed 1.5%; deterministic failure 0.8%. The numerical failure limits are illustrative, not safety standards. Irreversible harms, authorization, and other quality requirements require separate gates; expected cost cannot waive them.

Two decisions. One learning loop.

Choose investments on their expected contribution and the value of learning. Then choose execution routes by the full cost of an acceptable result. Set the appetite between them: the budget and quality boundary that keeps a promising bet from becoming unlimited work.

As generation gets cheaper, the constraint can move to review, integration, or judgment. A faster agent does not automatically produce more validated value. Observe what people actually do, compare the result with shared intent, and revise the next decision.

What these models can and cannot tell you. They are transparent sensitivity exercises, not measured forecasts or a validated operating system. The first compares a single uncertain bet; the second compares one representative task inside a bet. They are deliberately not numerically coupled: a representative task cannot establish the full task mix, review demand, calendar duration, or appetite for an entire product. This synthesis draws on Shape Up's appetite, Reinertsen's flow economics, and conventional Bayesian decision and value-of-information reasoning.
