The economics of AI-native product development.

When software becomes cheaper to produce, the advantage moves toward deciding what is worth making—and how best to make it.

More generated software is not necessarily more value. Human judgment, verification, integration, and failure remain costly. Two decisions help direct investment: choose a bet on the evidence, then choose the least costly acceptable way to execute it. Between them, define an appetite that bounds the work.

02 / Cost of execution

What is the least costly acceptable route?

The cheapest production is not always the cheapest acceptable result.

Once a bet is worth making, allocate its tasks within the appetite and quality floor. For one representative task, compare a direct agent, an agent with human review, a specialist, and a deterministic system. Exclude routes that cannot meet the stipulated constraints before comparing total expected cost.

Expected total = production + verification + delay + escaped-failure probability × loss. Here failure loss includes expected rework/recovery. All route base costs and durations are hypothetical: direct agent $24 / 0.1 day; reviewed agent $24 + selected review cost / 0.65 day; specialist $260 / 2 days; deterministic system $140 / 0.6 day. Specialist failure is assumed 1.5%; deterministic failure 0.8%. The numerical failure limits are illustrative, not safety standards. Irreversible harms, authorization, and other quality requirements require separate gates; expected cost cannot waive them.

Two decisions. One learning loop.

Choose investments on their expected contribution and the value of learning. Then choose execution routes by the full cost of an acceptable result. The appetite check between them makes the budget and quality boundary explicit, so a promising bet cannot become unlimited work.

As generation gets cheaper, the constraint can move to review, integration, or judgment. A faster agent does not automatically produce more validated value. Observe what people actually do, compare the result with shared intent, and revise the next decision.

What these models can and cannot tell you. They are transparent sensitivity exercises, not measured forecasts or a validated operating system. The first compares a single uncertain bet; the second compares one representative task inside a bet. The capacity illustration is not numerically derived from either decision: a representative task cannot establish an entire product’s task mix, review demand, critical path, or appetite. Its inputs are independent estimates. The four modeled limits are necessary, not sufficient, conditions for a viable release. This synthesis draws on Shape Up's appetite, Reinertsen's flow economics, and conventional Bayesian decision and value-of-information reasoning.
