The economics of AI-native product development.

When software becomes cheaper to produce, deciding what is worth producing—and how best to produce it—matters more than maximizing output.

Agents change the cost of making software. They do not eliminate the cost of choosing, verifying, integrating, or living with it. Two decisions govern the investment: how much the opportunity is worth, and which human–machine route can achieve an acceptable result at the lowest total expected cost.

01 / Commitment

What is this worth?

Choose the appetite before execution. Hold the commitment fixed; let the scope respond.

An appetite is a value judgment, not an estimate of an unlimited solution. In AI-native work, agent execution, human direction and review, money, and elapsed time require separate limits. Better agent productivity increases what might fit inside a bet. It does not retroactively enlarge the bet.

02 / Allocation

What is the least costly acceptable route?

The cheapest generation is not always the cheapest completed outcome.

A direct agent, a reviewed agent, a deterministic program, and a human specialist have different execution costs, latencies, and failure exposure. First rule out routes that cannot meet the task's safety and quality requirements. Then compare the remaining routes on total expected cost.

Two decisions. One economic discipline.

The first fixes what you're willing to invest. The second asks how to use that investment well. Both remain subordinate to the same test: did we produce the intended behavior, and did it create value in use?

More efficient execution can make a bad idea cheaper. It cannot make it worth doing. What happens in the world—not the volume of work generated—should inform the next bet.

About the models. Every rate, cost, and probability shown here is illustrative. These are decision instruments, not measured productivity forecasts or an empirically validated system.
