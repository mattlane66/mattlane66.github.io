Management Without Command

Command-and-control structures typically increase **transaction costs**: handoffs, sign-offs, lengthy and questionable meetings, etc. By distributing authority, you move decision-making downstream, which shrinks batch sizes and queue times—boosting the throughput of the overall system. High utilization without local authority leads to congestion (like traffic on a highway at capacity). **Allowing local decision-making on priorities** helps organizations adapt to variability before it grows into major bottlenecks.

**Management Without Command **keeps craftsmanship at the heart of production, ensuring that workers grow—and thrive—alongside the advancement of the “art” they collectively create.

Good management is not primarily tactical. It’s show, not tell. Ultimately, it’s about collective self-reliance. But in larger organizations, as a manager, you’re best positioned if you function as a mentor. Giving advice people can use, reviewing activities and the things people produce, and offering views on how to think. It is about teaching people how to think and redirect if things drift.

A talent-rich organization can let employees decide which initiatives they want to join. This is critical. It taps into intrinsic motivation because people commit more deeply to projects they *choose* rather than ones *assigned* to them.

- Each association should have a well-defined goal or theme, whether it’s a “task force” for improving remote collaboration tools or a “guild” for enhancing design practices.
- This ensures the group has a reason to exist and its members share a unifying interest.
- Associations thrive when they can define their roles, meeting schedules, and outputs.
- The company’s leadership can set basic guardrails (budget, mission alignment), i.e., mission-type tactics, but the group decides how to operate.
- While encouraging grassroots initiatives, leadership should help connect each group’s efforts to the company’s broader mission or strategic objectives and coach as needed.
- This ensures the work remains relevant and can receive organizational support (e.g., senior-level buy-in, expanded funding).
*The Result:* An internal culture that values and celebrates the artisan’s role as much as the overall progress of “the art.”
