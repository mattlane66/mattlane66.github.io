01If the universe is the answer, what is the question?Leon M. Lederman and Dick Teresi · Book subtitle · 1993+

The answer alone does not tell us what problem was being solved. Before treating an explanation as progress, ask which question it actually answers.

Exact wording of the subtitle of The God Particle (1993).

02The universe is not only stranger than we suppose; it may be stranger than we can suppose.J. B. S. Haldane · Possible Worlds · 1927+

The limitation might not be our lack of answers. It might be the range of possibilities we are able to imagine. What assumptions are we smuggling into the questions we ask?

Paraphrase. Haldane's original uses “queerer,” not “stranger.” The familiar wording has several later variants.

03Can the motion of the world be as much my activity as the beating of my heart?Alan Watts · Eastern Wisdom and Modern Life, episode 13 · 1959+
If I do not will the beating of my heart, might I also feel that the clouds, the winds, the circling stars, the tides, and the flowing rivers—none of which I will—are nevertheless as much my activity as the blood moving through my veins?

Watts asks where the boundary of the self should be drawn. The thought can change how the world feels without establishing that every event is literally an act of personal agency.

Adapted wording supplied for this collection; not a verbatim transcript.

04What was it like to wake up after having never gone to sleep?Alan Watts · Out of Your Mind, The Nature of Consciousness · 1969+

Watts turns the fear of never waking into a question about birth. We can imagine a world without us, but not an experience of our own non-experience. That distinction invites reflection; it does not establish a theory of what happens after death.

Phrase appears in the recorded lecture's published transcript.

05Is the pious loved because it is pious, or pious because it is loved by the gods?Plato · Euthyphro 10a · Socrates' question+

Does an authority recognize something as good, or make it good by approving it? The two explanations have different consequences. The question still applies when the authority is a leader, institution, or majority.

Close paraphrase of Plato's Euthyphro, 10a; the wording varies by translation.

06If you break the line of word and image, what happens?Unattributed · Question supplied for this collection+

Words can tell us what to notice in an image. Images can make words feel true without proving them. Remove their usual agreement and ask what changes in meaning, interpretation, or judgment.

No reliable source for this exact formulation was established. It is presented as a question, not a quotation from a named thinker.

07If the Sun seems to circle the Earth, how would it look if the Earth turned?Ludwig Wittgenstein · Reported by G. E. M. Anscombe · 1959+

It can feel natural to say, “I know I am in pain.” It once seemed natural to think the Sun circled the Earth: after all, that was how it looked. But how would things look if the Earth were turning instead? Two distinct Wittgensteinian prompts challenge what our ordinary ways of speaking and seeing actually establish.

Composite paraphrase, not one continuous quote. The pain discussion is in Philosophical Investigations §246; Anscombe reports the Sun/Earth exchange in An Introduction to Wittgenstein’s Tractatus, p. 151.

08Should society preserve jobs, or maximize production and create abundance?Unattributed · Question supplied for this collection+

Work provides income, identity, and a place in society. Production can increase while employment falls. The question forces us to ask which benefits we want to preserve, who receives them, and whether jobs are the only way to provide them.

No verified source for this exact wording. This is a policy question, not an attributed quotation or an automatic argument for eliminating jobs.

01 / Ground it

Start with something that could happen.

A concept becomes useful when it changes what you notice or what you do. Choose a situation. Follow the different lines of reasoning. See what remains unproved.

02 / Put ideas beside each other

Where do they actually disagree?

Some theories collide. Some fill different gaps. Some cannot be compared without changing the question. Each pairing names the relationship rather than inventing an argument.

Choose your own pair

Where do these thinkers meet?

A shared question is a place to compare, not a claim that two thinkers disagreed. Direct contrasts appear only when we've written and checked them.

03 / Check the inference

Where does the argument make a leap?

A fact can support a conclusion without proving it. These chains are constructed examples inspired by the thinkers, not reconstructions of arguments they jointly made. Follow each step and inspect the premise in between.

04 / Follow the questions

The connections are the map.

Select a question to reveal its neighbors. An edge suggests a useful next question, not a proof that one answer entails another.

05 / Look closer

Twenty questions. No final answers.

Each card gives a working answer, a situation that brings the question down to earth, and the strongest disagreement. Open the detail to follow thinkers and sources.

06 / Reference

The thinkers behind the questions.

Fifty-six people, each with a representative work, core idea, significance, and qualification. The map isn't a vote, a canon, or a claim that every contribution has equal evidence.

A mathematical proof is not a metaphysical argument. An experimental result is not a novel. A developmental stage theory is not established merely because it tells an orderly story. The kind of claim matters.
