An Experimental Governance Model

A Possible World Model for All

No one fully authors their starting position. Family, health, talents, inherited wealth, neighborhood, and the institutions surrounding us shape what we can become. A just society should not allow this luck to harden into deprivation, inherited caste, or power that places some people at the mercy of others.

Yet people need agency, responsibility, ambition, risk, enterprise, disagreement, and room to build things no one anticipated. Justice cannot require identical outcomes or one institution governing every part of life.

The aim is to civilize luck without abolishing agency: secure the conditions of equal standing, prevent domination, reward productive contribution, and preserve the freedom to experiment. This requires markets capable of discovery, a state capable of securing rights, associations capable of giving people voice, and checks on the power of each.

Private domination is real. State domination is real. The test must work against both.

What follows is a political philosophy and a method for choosing among imperfect institutions. The philosophy supplies commitments. The method tests particular arrangements—including the status quo—against realistic alternatives. A failed policy does not refute the commitments, and an attractive intention does not establish a sound policy.

Equal standing, not equal outcomes

People can be held responsible in the practical sense: they respond to reasons, obligations, incentives, and consequences. Institutions should cultivate that capacity through opportunity, accountability, prevention, repair, and reintegration. But practical responsibility does not make anyone wholly self-made. Unchosen circumstances still shape the opportunities from which people choose.

If a political community claims to treat people as equals, it cannot be indifferent when circumstances of birth determine who can learn, work, bargain, participate, or leave an abusive relationship. The obligation is not to eliminate luck. It is to stop luck from becoming a durable structure of subordination.

Inequality is not, by itself, an injustice. Two artists may earn very different incomes from voluntary purchases without either wronging the other. The relevant test is principally institutional: do the rules permitting unequal rewards secure the floor, preserve fair opportunity and equal civic standing, and prevent wealth from becoming arbitrary command over others?

Exceptional rewards may be justified by contribution, scarcity, innovation, judgment, or risk. They need not be individually proved to benefit the worst-off. But the overall rules governing rewards, inheritance, competition, taxation, labor, and political influence must remain answerable to the position of the least advantaged. This is more demanding than simply accepting whatever outcomes a market produces.

Similarly, dependency is not domination. Dependence on a hospital, a power grid, or income support can increase freedom when access is reliable and decisions are accountable. Nor does founder control automatically amount to domination; employee ownership does not automatically prevent it. The operative questions are: What can an authority decide? What limits bind it? Can affected people realistically challenge it? What remedy follows abuse? Exit matters, but an exit that is legally available and materially impossible is not much protection.

These obligations start with those subject to a political community's institutions, including noncitizen residents whose lives those institutions govern. Citizenship does not determine whether someone has basic human rights. Cross-border effects create duties not to impose avoidable serious harm on outsiders; future generations have claims against institutions that consume the resources and capacities on which their security will depend. This model does not settle global distributive justice. It cannot use political borders to erase consequences beyond them.

Commitments with different jobs

The framework has three moral aims and one practical requirement. They are not four interchangeable scores.

Rights set limits

Freedom of speech, conscience, association, due process, equal protection, political participation, and effective ways to contest arbitrary decisions constrain what institutions may do. An income gain does not automatically justify surveillance, discrimination, coerced silence, or denial of appeal. Rights require interpretation when they conflict, but they are not simply entries in a cost-benefit spreadsheet.

A material floor must be secured

People need reliable conditions for participation: sufficient material security, healthcare, housing stability, nutrition, education, energy, transportation, connectivity, and credible routes into productive work. A floor is a guarantee of effective access, not necessarily a state monopoly over provision or an identical bundle in every place.

Power and equal standing guide the choice

Within the boundaries set by rights and the obligation to secure the floor, institutions should widen opportunity, constrain arbitrary authority, protect genuine exit and voice, and avoid inherited civic hierarchy. The distribution of benefits, burdens, and risks matters, particularly for people with the least bargaining power.

Capacity makes promises real

Every proposal must be financially sustainable, administrable, enforceable, and open to correction. Productive capacity, political incentives, and institutional competence are not substitutes for justice. They are conditions for delivering it. A guarantee that survives only on paper is not a guarantee.

These distinctions allow real tradeoffs. Some additional complexity may be justified by large gains in security or freedom. Other designs fail because their intended benefits depend on discretion that cannot be bounded, supply that cannot be produced, or commitments that cannot be maintained.

A pluralist political economy

Markets discover needs, coordinate dispersed knowledge, reward useful experiments, and mobilize investment. They also operate through laws, property rights, infrastructure, public goods, and institutions they did not create by themselves. Keeping markets creative means making their terms contestable and preventing competition from turning into durable privilege.

The state must secure rights and floors, enforce competition, address harms, and provide or finance what other institutions cannot reliably supply. It must also be limited by law, independent review, transparency, and effective remedies. Associations, unions, cooperatives, communities, nonprofits, and firms can create belonging, voice, and initiative that neither governments nor markets should monopolize.

I call the proposed direction Capitalism 6.0: a pluralist market democracy in which productive success is rewarded but cannot purchase unchecked authority, public risks come with public terms, and universal guarantees coexist with diverse local and private delivery. The name is a proposal, not a claim that capitalism has passed through five agreed historical stages.

This is not a demand for state ownership of everything, equal outcomes, or permanent founder disempowerment. It is a demand that power—public or private—remain bounded, contestable, and accountable.

A permanent floor requires a productive economy

Cash cannot house someone where no home is available. Insurance cannot deliver care where there are no clinicians. A subsidy cannot provide reliable transport where service does not exist. A material floor therefore depends on producing and delivering enough of the goods and services that make participation possible, not only funding claims to them.

That means assessing investment incentives, competition, land use, labor supply, infrastructure, permitting, training, technology, procurement, and delivery capacity. Public provision, nonprofit institutions, regulated private markets, or hybrids may be appropriate in different settings. No ownership form earns an exemption from the test of performance.

Scarcity also shapes distribution. An access subsidy in a severely supply-constrained market may raise purchasing power without producing enough additional service. Supply expansion without protection can displace existing residents or impose real local costs. A capable institution must attend to both.

Build the floor for recessions, inflation, demographic change, fiscal pressure, trade shocks, and failures of delivery. Specify stable financing, automatic stabilization where appropriate, and contingency plans for supply shortfalls. Preserve incentives for investment, entrepreneurship, learning, and movement into more productive work. A floor that destroys its own means of support is fragile justice.

The guarantee should endure even when its delivery mechanism changes. Review, replace, shrink, or end an ineffective program without interrupting essential security. A sunset clause may discipline a pilot or an administrative instrument; it should not make the underlying floor contingent on recurring political bargaining. And because some choices—education, infrastructure, relocation—cannot literally be reversed, require proportionate opportunities for correction, transition, compensation, and remedy instead of promising universal reversibility.

Prefer simple, legible, automatic arrangements when they work; permit local adaptation within universal rights and minimum guarantees. A system should be no more complex than its responsibilities require.

Enterprise, worker voice, and ownership

Founders organize talent, take risks, make judgments, and bring new possibilities into existence. These contributions deserve rewards, including substantial wealth and sometimes durable control. But successful enterprises also rely on workers, suppliers, customers, shared infrastructure, public law, and the knowledge accumulated across the firm. Neither fact settles its governance.

The question is not whether every workplace should become a town meeting. It is whether people can be subject to arbitrary decisions about livelihood, safety, speech, and opportunity without meaningful constraints or recourse. Worker voice, bargaining rights, portable benefits, fair dismissal procedures, profit-sharing, board representation, and ownership all address different parts of that problem.

Broadening access to productive assets is a valuable aim, but wealth-building, worker voice, and managerial accountability are three separate tests. A reform can improve one and fail another. Compare each proposed structure with simpler instruments that accomplish the same purpose.

An Employee Ownership Trust as a candidate, not a presumption

An Employee Ownership Trust could hold a meaningful or controlling stake on workers' behalf while leaving day-to-day decisions with accountable management. It may be attractive for a mature firm with stable cash flow and a founder seeking succession. The possible advantages are shared wealth, continuity, and employee representation without forcing each worker to hold or trade individual shares.

But a trust concentrates legal ownership in trustees. Its legitimacy depends on who appoints and removes them, who sees financial information, how employees elect or challenge representatives, how minority and dissenting workers are protected, and what prevents managers from controlling the institution meant to hold them accountable. Clear limits on managerial discretion matter as much as voting arrangements.

Keep current profit distributions, accumulated or vested capital claims, and governance rights conceptually distinct. Test departure and vesting terms against the worker's practical ability to exit. Workers whose wages and wealth both depend on one employer face concentrated risk. Diversification, liquidity, leverage, valuation, and firm-failure protections matter.

Compare a trust with ordinary profit-sharing, an employee stock plan, a cooperative, a works council, collective bargaining, or stronger worker protections without any ownership transfer. If a simpler arrangement improves voice or security with less risk, choose it. Do not compel one template across every firm. Enable voluntary adoption and evaluate actual outcomes.

Public risk should come with public terms

Private firms properly keep returns from productive risks they bear. But when the public absorbs extraordinary downside through rescues, guarantees, subsidies, procurement, or protected markets, the terms should reflect that exposure. Depending on the case, the public may require repayment, warrants, revenue sharing, enforceable service commitments, clawbacks, or demonstrable long-term public benefits.

The test is proportionality: what risk was actually transferred, what public value can be verified, what incentives are created, and who can enforce the agreement? Public upside is not automatic public ownership. A condition that discourages socially useful investment or creates political favoritism can fail the same test.

Finance should support durable productive capacity and broad opportunity rather than reward extraction while shifting losses to workers, communities, taxpayers, and future generations.

Candidate experiments, not a fixed package

The philosophy does not establish a single correct institutional form. It does suggest reforms worth comparing with existing arrangements and simpler alternatives. Among the candidates are:

  • Material security: universal child benefits, a negative income tax, basic health coverage, housing and transit provision, connectivity, and energy resilience—using different funding and delivery models where appropriate.
  • Voice and assets: works councils, collective bargaining, portable benefits, profit-sharing, worker ownership, and cooperative or community investment vehicles, each judged against its specific purpose.
  • Competitive production and finance: enforcement against exclusionary market power, patient capital for productive investment, support for firm succession, and conditions when public institutions bear private risk.
  • Accountable government: appeal rights, independent audits, anti-corruption safeguards, bounded agency discretion, automatic stabilizers, and time-limited trials where continuity of essential protections is secured.

These are hypotheses, not entitlements to enactment. Their value depends on the problem, the institutional setting, and evidence of results. They can complement, compete with, or outperform one another.

How to choose among imperfect institutions

The burden of proof applies to every feasible option, including continued operation of the existing system. Inaction has consequences; familiar institutions do not become neutral merely by being familiar.

  1. Define the problem and the guarantee. Identify the affected people, the harm, the minimum condition to be secured, and the rights that cannot be traded away. Specify whose interests are missing from current decisions, including people excluded from the system.
  2. Compare feasible alternatives. Include the present arrangement, incremental repair, and at least one materially different approach. Describe how each is expected to work, what evidence supports that mechanism, and where evidence is weak. Count the cost of delay as well as the cost of reform.
  3. Apply binding constraints. Reject options that violate rights or leave basic protections without a credible path to fulfillment. Do not justify arbitrary power by pointing to aggregate gains. Where rights or guarantees genuinely conflict, make the conflict and the principled resolution explicit.
  4. Judge comparative consequences. Examine security, opportunity, productive capacity, incentives, fiscal durability, environmental and intergenerational effects, and the distribution of gains and losses. Ask whether manageable complexity or dependency is worth the benefit; neither is disqualifying by definition.
  5. Specify the authority and the remedy. Who makes which decisions? Under what rules? Who funds, implements, and audits? What information must be public? Can affected people challenge an error or abuse, and obtain an effective remedy? How will capture be detected?
  6. Test, review, and correct. Choose measures before scaling; include harms that are difficult to quantify. Use bounded trials when ethically and practically possible. Establish independent review, clear decision points, and means to adjust, compensate, replace, or stop failing instruments while maintaining any underlying guarantee.

The aim is not to find a policy with no costs. It is to choose the defensible alternative, make the costs visible, and preserve the institutional means to correct mistakes. Evidence should inform democratic judgment, not displace it.

A decision in practice: housing

Consider an illustrative high-demand city where housing costs consume too much household income, vacancies are scarce, and building new homes is difficult. Assume local evidence confirms these conditions. This is a decision exercise, not an empirical verdict about a particular city.

The existing arrangement: restrictive land-use rules, long approvals, limited new supply, and targeted assistance. Existing homeowners retain substantial veto power. Current residents may value neighborhood continuity, but renters, future residents, and workers priced out of the area bear costs that are less visible in local hearings. Maintaining the arrangement is a choice with its own burdens.

Alternative A: prioritize immediate tenant security through targeted assistance, enforceable protections against displacement and harassment, and rent stabilization where warranted, with incremental public or nonprofit construction. This can protect existing residents, and additional nonmarket housing can expand access. Its limits depend on funding, implementation capacity, and whether incremental supply can meet demand in a market constrained by land-use rules.

Alternative B: permit substantially more homes where infrastructure can support them, make compliant projects easier to approve, and pair the change with enforceable anti-displacement protections and targeted assistance. This seeks to expand physical supply while protecting those exposed to transition costs. Public or nonprofit construction can complement private building where evidence shows persistent gaps, but it too must meet capacity and fiscal tests.

A provisional choice—and a rejection

Provisionally favor B as the primary long-run response if credible local evidence shows restrictions on construction are a central cause of scarcity and the city can deliver its promised infrastructure and protections. Keep tenant safeguards and, where effective, nonmarket construction from A. This does not establish that B is always superior: where building capacity is severely constrained or immediate displacement is the dominant harm, stronger elements of A may deserve priority. Reject an indiscriminate, supply-blind rent-cap expansion as a complete solution, even while recognizing the security it may provide to some tenants.

This choice accepts construction disruption, administrative costs, and some changes to neighborhood character in return for the prospect of greater long-run access. It cannot ignore displacement, environmental constraints, property rights, or residents' legitimate voice. But local voice should not become an unlimited veto held only by those fortunate enough to already live there.

The relevant decision-makers should measure permits and completed homes, rents relative to income, availability by income band, involuntary displacement, homelessness, build times, infrastructure performance, fiscal cost, and distribution of new access. Residents need transparent rules and meaningful appeals against unlawful approvals, discrimination, displacement, and agency abuse; appeals should not simply recreate an unbounded veto.

What could overturn the choice? Evidence that restrictions are not the binding constraint; inadequate construction capacity; severe unmitigated displacement; infrastructure failures; or costs that defeat the promised access. In that event, revise the mix rather than defending the chosen instrument as an article of faith. The guarantee—people's practical access to secure housing—remains the point.

Evidence without technocracy

Measurement is necessary. Measurement is not sovereignty. The model needs observable tests for its claims about the floor, power, status, and institutional capacity. No single indicator can settle any of them.

  • Floor: deep and child poverty; housing burden and homelessness; untreated illness and food insecurity; access to education, transport, energy, and connectivity; emergency savings; and the actual supply of essential goods and services.
  • Power: employer and market concentration; restrictions on mobility; creditor, landlord, and platform dependence; political and procurement concentration; and whether people can challenge decisions and secure remedies.
  • Status: intergenerational mobility; inherited disadvantage; discriminatory outcomes in housing, credit, employment, healthcare, and education; civic participation; and persistent exclusion from opportunity.
  • Capacity: fiscal resilience; administrative cost and processing time; error, fraud, and appeal outcomes; infrastructure and delivery performance; enforcement consistency; conflicts of interest; and institutional capture.

These are candidate indicators, not a universal dashboard to maximize. For each decision, establish a baseline, a feasible comparator including inaction, the expected time to see change, and the people who might gain or lose. Specify in advance what would justify continuation, correction, or withdrawal. Examine absolute outcomes as well as differences between groups.

Interpret the measures rather than worship them. Concentration may reveal a risk of domination without proving arbitrary power. A low appeal-success rate might mean sound initial decisions—or an inaccessible appeal process. A reported improvement in average income can conceal exclusion, surveillance, or lost freedom. Quantitative results should be read alongside people's experience and evidence about institutional mechanisms.

The selection of metrics is itself a political decision. Affected people should be able to challenge definitions, inspect evidence consistent with privacy and due process, and contest claims of success. Independent auditors need authority and protections against capture. Long delays, hard-to-measure harms, spillovers, and uncertainty should appear in the evaluation rather than be hidden behind a single score.

Experiments must be proportionate to the risk. A local administrative change may justify a controlled pilot; a structural change to essential services may require parallel delivery, staged adoption, continuity guarantees, and remedies before any trial. Neither reversibility nor a sunset date is a universal virtue. Correctability is.

Review the prevailing arrangement as seriously as the innovation. If an intervention repeatedly fails, revise or withdraw it. If the existing institution continues to impose avoidable deprivation, domination, or exclusion, doing nothing also fails the test.

The purpose of the model

The task is not to abolish markets, deny responsibility, flatten differences, or entrust justice to a master institution. It is to create a pluralist political economy where people can stand as equals without surrendering the agency that makes their lives their own.

Protect rights. Secure the floor. Produce the things the floor requires. Prevent public and private domination. Let enterprise create value without converting success into unchecked command. Compare institutions with their real alternatives, then revise what fails.

The philosophy defines what must matter. The experiment discovers what works. Neither excuses the other.
