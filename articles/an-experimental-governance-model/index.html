An Experimental Governance Model

A Possible World Model For All

Because no one fully authors their starting position, talents, family, health, inherited wealth, local environment, or social conditions, a just society should not allow arbitrary luck to harden into deprivation, domination, inherited caste, or second-class citizenship.

But because people still need agency, responsibility, pluralism, entrepreneurship, voluntary association, experimentation, and incentives, justice should not mean flattening all difference or centralizing all control.

The right goal is to civilize luck: secure universal floors, prevent domination, broaden ownership, preserve democratic voice, protect liberty, and keep institutions simple, adaptive, and locally responsive.

Inequalities are acceptable only when they raise or protect the floor, preserve equal civic standing, and do not give some people arbitrary power over others.

This is not anti-market. It is an attempt to update capitalism itself: to keep markets where they generate discovery, innovation, competition, coordination, experimentation, and productive risk-taking, while redesigning the rules so that market power does not become caste power, monopoly power, employer domination, landlord domination, cronyism, political capture, or a system of privatized gains and socialized losses.

It is also not a blank check for the state. A model that worries about private domination must worry just as seriously about public domination. The state can protect people from employers, landlords, creditors, monopolies, platforms, and concentrated capital, but it can also become coercive, captured, opaque, punitive, paternalistic, or self-preserving.

The model therefore begins from a principle of anti-domination symmetry:

Private domination is real. State domination is real. A just political economy must prevent both.

A simple rule governs the model:

Keep what raises the floor and does not dominate. Reform or remove what concentrates power, externalizes risk, blocks participation, destroys exit, creates arbitrary dependency, or gives public or private actors unchecked discretion over other people’s lives.

This model does not treat every inequality, hierarchy, dependency, or unpleasant bargain as domination.

Domination means a durable and hard-to-exit form of power that is insufficiently accountable and capable of arbitrarily shaping another person’s life chances, voice, security, opportunity, or civic standing.

This model is not a demand for equal outcomes, state ownership of everything, hostility to entrepreneurship, or the abolition of markets. Nor does it claim that luck eliminates responsibility. It claims that responsibility should operate inside institutions that do not convert arbitrary starting points into permanent vulnerability, exclusion, or subordination.

The policy proposals below are candidate reforms, not a single package that must be adopted wholesale. Each reform must carry its own burden of proof. It must show that it improves floor, power, or status outcomes without creating worse domination, dependency, administrative complexity, fiscal fragility, institutional capture, or state overreach.

Human beings are responsible in the practical sense: they respond to reasons, incentives, relationships, institutions, obligations, and expectations. A just society should preserve this kind of agency. It should hold people accountable, reward effort and contribution, and design institutions that encourage responsibility.

But people are not ultimately self-made. Their capacities, dispositions, opportunities, family background, health, inherited wealth or poverty, local environment, and social networks are heavily shaped by factors they did not choose.

Therefore, institutions should not convert brute luck into deprivation, humiliation, caste status, blocked capability, or unchecked power.

This does not mean that every unequal outcome is unjust. It means that institutions should be judged by whether they allow unchosen starting points to become permanent vulnerability or domination.

Core rule: responsibility should be forward-looking: prevention, repair, incentive design, accountability, and reintegration, rather than metaphysical blame or permanent exclusion.

The fact that people do not choose their starting conditions does not by itself prove any particular policy.

The missing bridge is this: if a political community claims to treat its members as equals, then it cannot be indifferent when unchosen circumstances predict whether people can participate, bargain, learn, work, exit abusive dependencies, or stand before institutions as peers.

A society can tolerate differences in talent, effort, taste, ambition, risk tolerance, reward, and life choices. It cannot legitimately allow accidents of birth, inherited caste, monopoly power, institutional capture, or state abuse to decide who is secure and who is disposable.

The obligation is not to erase all luck. That is impossible.

The obligation is to prevent luck from becoming a permanent structure of subordination.

Every person must be treated as an equal member of the political community.

Unequal outcomes are not automatically unjust. They become unjust when they produce deprivation, domination, blocked capability, inherited caste, humiliation, political exclusion, or second-class citizenship.

Core rule: inequality is permissible only when it is compatible with equal standing.

Equal standing means more than formal legal equality. It requires that people have enough material security, rights, voice, and exit power to avoid being at the mercy of employers, landlords, creditors, monopolies, political insiders, platforms, communities, associations, or the state.

The model does not treat all inequality as domination.

Some inequalities can be legitimate when they reflect real contribution, innovation, risk, scarcity, responsibility, talent, or productive coordination, especially when they improve conditions for others and remain compatible with equal civic status.

For example, paying doctors more than many other workers can be justified if the higher pay helps attract and retain skilled doctors, expands access, shortens wait times, or brings care to poor and rural communities. The inequality is justified only because it helps raise the floor. If higher pay simply enriches an already protected professional class while access worsens for the least advantaged, the inequality fails the test.

Other inequalities are illegitimate because they allow some people to command the lives, opportunities, speech, housing, work, political influence, or institutional standing of others without adequate accountability.

The question is not simply:

Are outcomes unequal?

The better questions are:

  • Did this inequality arise through productive contribution or through extraction, monopoly, inherited gatekeeping, political capture, coercive dependence, or public risk-bearing without public upside?
  • Does it raise or protect the floor?
  • Does it preserve real opportunity and exit power?
  • Does it convert wealth into unchecked social, economic, or political command?
  • Can those affected contest the decision rules that govern them?
  • Does correcting the inequality require creating even greater state discretion or institutional fragility?

Executive compensation is a harder test case. High CEO pay may be justified if it is part of a system that builds durable firms, raises wages, improves training, expands opportunity, and benefits lower-paid workers. But if executives receive large bonuses while workers face stagnant pay, weaker benefits, insecurity, or arbitrary control, then the inequality looks less like productive reward and more like concentrated power.

A policy, market rule, institution, or ownership structure is acceptable only if it passes four guardrails.

Does it secure basic capabilities and reduce severe vulnerability?

A just society must ensure that no one is locked out of participation because they lack basic income security, healthcare, housing stability, transportation, education, nutrition, internet access, energy access, or realistic pathways into productive work.

Does it prevent domination by employers, landlords, monopolies, creditors, political insiders, platforms, associations, communities, or the state?

The model must prevent both private domination and public domination. A reform that reduces corporate abuse but creates unchecked bureaucratic discretion has not automatically succeeded. A reform that limits state discretion but leaves people at the mercy of employers, landlords, creditors, monopolies, or platforms has also not succeeded.

Does it avoid caste, humiliation, exclusion, inherited subordination, and second-class citizenship?

A society can have unequal roles, unequal rewards, and unequal outcomes. It cannot allow people to be treated as lesser members of the political community.

Can the institution actually be administered competently, transparently, and with limited discretion?

A reform is not justified merely because it advances floor, power, or status goals. It must also be administrable, fundable, enforceable, auditable, reversible, and resistant to capture.

It must specify:

  • who holds authority
  • who funds it
  • who enforces it
  • who audits it
  • who can appeal
  • who can veto abuse
  • how discretion is bounded
  • how conflicts of interest are blocked
  • how capture is detected
  • how failure is measured
  • how the institution can be revised, shrunk, or ended

The first three tests define the moral aims. The fourth test asks whether the machinery built to pursue those aims can survive contact with real politics, real incentives, real bureaucracy, real scarcity, and bad-faith actors.

These tests apply not only to welfare policy, but also to labor markets, corporate governance, finance, housing, infrastructure, education, healthcare, taxation, ownership law, procurement, regulation, and democratic institutions.

Capitalism has evolved through several broad phases: early market capitalism, industrial capitalism, corporate capitalism, welfare-state capitalism, and neoliberal or state-enabled corporate capitalism.

The next update should not abolish markets, but discipline them around justice.

Capitalism 6.0 means market institutions designed around three constraints:

  1. They must raise or protect the floor.
  1. They must not create domination.
  1. They must remain institutionally governable, contestable, and resistant to capture.

This means fighting crony capitalism, monopoly concentration, financial structures that privatize gains while socializing losses, and ownership systems that allow a small group to permanently command the productive lives of many others.

The point is not equal outcomes.

The point is equal standing, broad opportunity, and a political economy where productive success does not become unchecked social power.

A real floor is not just cash. People need the basic infrastructure of participation.

That includes:

  • Basic income support, such as a universal child benefit and negative income tax.
  • Basic healthcare, including catastrophic coverage and preventive care.
  • Affordable and sustainable housing.
  • Reliable transportation, including rural-to-urban access where needed.
  • Universal high-speed internet.
  • Basic nutritional security.
  • Rapid retraining pathways for workers displaced by technology, trade, or industrial change.
  • Strong public education that develops critical thinking, civic competence, and practical adaptability.
  • Affordable clean energy and resilient infrastructure.

The goal is not to make everyone the same. The goal is to ensure that no one is locked out of participation because they lack the basic tools of modern life.

Opportunity infrastructure should be universal in floor, plural in delivery, and locally adaptable in implementation.

Local communities, associations, religious institutions, mutual-aid networks, cooperatives, nonprofits, and private actors may exceed, supplement, or customize public opportunity infrastructure. But they may not opt out of universal rights, due process, child protection, anti-discrimination, or minimum floor guarantees.

Prefer policies that are legible, hard to game, locally adaptable, and automatically corrected when conditions change.

Design preferences:

  • Simple before complex.
  • Universal before stigmatizing.
  • Automatic before discretionary.
  • Modular before monolithic.
  • Feedback-driven before set-and-forget.
  • Anti-capture by default.
  • Local adaptation within universal rights.
  • Rights before dashboards.
  • Sunset, review, and revision before permanent institutional expansion.
  • Clear operating rules before broad delegation.
  • Institutional capacity before moral ambition.

Because social systems are complex and human knowledge is limited, the model should avoid utopian blueprints. It should use experimental institutions, measurable guardrails, limited discretion, public accountability, and periodic review.

Every major reform should include an operating constitution before it is scaled.

That operating constitution should answer:

  • What exact problem does this reform solve?
  • Which guardrail does it primarily serve: floor, power, status, or institutional capacity?
  • Who has legal authority?
  • Who pays?
  • Who benefits?
  • Who might be harmed?
  • Who enforces compliance?
  • What discretion does the institution have?
  • What discretion is forbidden?
  • What rights cannot be traded away?
  • What data will be collected?
  • Who audits the data?
  • How can affected people appeal?
  • How are conflicts of interest prevented?
  • What would count as success?
  • What would count as failure?
  • What happens automatically if it fails?
  • How does the reform end, shrink, or change?

No reform should be judged by intentions alone. It should be judged by outcomes, side effects, enforceability, reversibility, and resistance to capture.

No single institution should dominate society.

The state, markets, associations, communities, ownership structures, courts, and civil liberties should check and balance one another.

The state should set floors, rights, competition rules, and anti-domination constraints.

Markets should coordinate discovery, production, innovation, and decentralized experimentation.

Associations should give people collective voice, especially at work and in local communities.

Ownership should be broadly distributed so wealth-building is not confined to a narrow asset-owning class.

Communities should build trust, belonging, and mutual obligation.

Rights should limit all of them.

Core balance: strong public rules, competitive markets, broad ownership, empowered associations, protected liberties, and institutional checks against domination from any direction.

A model that worries about private domination must also worry about public domination.

The state can protect people from employers, landlords, monopolies, creditors, platforms, and concentrated capital. But it can also become coercive, captured, opaque, punitive, paternalistic, or self-protective.

So the state should be strong enough to secure rights and floors, but limited enough that it cannot become the master institution of society.

Safeguards should include:

  • Constitutional and civil-liberty protections.
  • Due process against administrative abuse.
  • Transparent rulemaking and public reasons.
  • Independent courts and review bodies.
  • Decentralized implementation where appropriate.
  • Local experimentation within universal rights.
  • Anti-corruption and conflict-of-interest rules.
  • Sunset clauses and periodic reauthorization for major programs.
  • Public audits of capture, complexity, exclusion, and coercive side effects.
  • The right to appeal decisions made by public agencies or publicly funded intermediaries.
  • Legible rules that minimize unnecessary discretion.
  • Clear separation between funding, enforcement, auditing, and political influence.

The aim is not a bigger state for its own sake. The aim is a state capable of preventing domination without becoming a source of domination itself.

Entrepreneurship is valuable.

Founders create, organize, take risks, solve problems, and bring new possibilities into the world. A just economy should reward that.

But business success is rarely the product of one person alone. It depends on workers, customers, suppliers, public infrastructure, law, education systems, communications networks, transportation systems, energy systems, courts, money, trust, and the wider society that makes enterprise possible.

So entrepreneurs should be rewarded for creation, judgment, coordination, risk, and leadership. But founder reward should not automatically justify perpetual, exclusive control over the people who sustain the enterprise over time.

A better model would preserve entrepreneurial incentives while preventing ownership from becoming domination.

Principles for fair entrepreneurship:

  • Founders may receive substantial early rewards, recognition, control, and upside.
  • Investors may earn returns for taking real risk and providing patient capital.
  • Exceptional builders should be able to earn exceptional rewards when they create exceptional value.
  • Workers who sustain the enterprise should have pathways to share in wealth creation and, where feasible, governance.
  • Public or cooperative financing can reduce the need for reckless private risk-taking.
  • Ownership should transition over time toward broader participation where feasible and where it does not destroy the productive function of the firm.
  • Extreme rewards are not justified when they depend on monopoly, extraction, worker powerlessness, political capture, or public risk-bearing without public upside.

The question is not whether entrepreneurship should be rewarded. It should be.

The question is whether entrepreneurial reward must take the form of permanent control, unlimited extraction, or unchecked command over others. It does not.

The fact that workers contribute to a firm does not automatically prove that every firm should be governed like a town meeting.

Different firms require different leadership structures, speed, expertise, confidentiality, decisiveness, and risk allocation.

But worker contribution does create a claim against complete exclusion from the rules that shape workers’ lives, income, safety, dignity, and long-term prospects.

The strongest case is not that every decision should be democratized. The strongest case is that workers should have meaningful voice, fair exit options, transparent information, due process, and a share in the value they help create.

Worker participation can take different forms depending on firm size, sector, complexity, capital needs, stage of development, and worker preference:

  • Profit-sharing.
  • Gain-sharing.
  • Employee stock ownership.
  • Employee Ownership Trusts.
  • Works councils.
  • Board representation in larger firms.
  • Sectoral bargaining.
  • Portable benefits.
  • Open-book management where feasible.
  • Due-process protections against arbitrary dismissal or retaliation.

The aim is not to eliminate leadership. The aim is to prevent ownership and management from becoming unchecked command.

Worker voice should improve information, trust, accountability, and shared upside. It should not require every operational decision to become plebiscitary, slow, or politically paralyzed.

A just economy should not rely only on redistribution after the fact. It should distribute productive power more broadly from the start.

That means expanding ownership models that give workers and communities a real stake in the firms and assets they help sustain, including:

  • Employee Ownership Trusts.
  • Worker cooperatives.
  • Employee Stock Ownership Plans where appropriate.
  • Steward-ownership models.
  • Cooperative finance.
  • Community Investment Trusts.
  • Public investment banks or mission-oriented financing vehicles, such as the Bank of North Dakota or WIR Bank.
  • Profit-sharing and broad-based capital accounts.

Broad ownership is a preferred direction of institutional evolution, not a universal mandate.

The state should remove barriers, create templates, finance transitions, support voluntary adoption, and penalize extractive structures. But it should not force every firm into the same governance form.

Broad ownership should be encouraged, not imposed as a single template on every enterprise. The appropriate form depends on firm size, capital needs, risk profile, sector, competitive environment, worker preference, and the productive function of the firm.

The goal is not uniformity. The goal is a pluralist ownership ecology that gives more people access to wealth-building, voice, and exit power.

High-quality workers often possess practical knowledge about operations, customers, risks, and improvements. Good governance should use that knowledge rather than exclude it.

One promising institutional form is a simplified, next-generation Employee Ownership Trust.

The trust would hold a controlling or significant stake in the company on behalf of employees. Instead of requiring every worker to manage individual shares directly, the trust would centralize ownership while distributing financial upside and governance rights.

A strong version could include:

  • Automatic participation for all employees.
  • A trust that holds a controlling or significant ownership stake.
  • Profit-sharing for current workers on a vesting schedule.
  • Individual capital accounts or long-term wealth-building mechanisms.
  • Employee-elected representation in governance.
  • Transparent financial reporting and open-book management where feasible.
  • Founder transition incentives.
  • Clear valuation rules.
  • Protections against demutualization or sale without supermajority approval.
  • Standardized legal templates to reduce complexity.
  • Fiduciary duties that balance worker benefit, firm resilience, and long-term mission.
  • Protection for dissenting workers and minority views.
  • Clear executive authority for operational decisions.
  • Defined procedures for resolving conflicts between worker benefit, firm survival, and long-term investment.

This model could be simpler than traditional ESOPs while still giving employees meaningful economic participation and voice. It would also allow founders to exit or partially cash out without selling the company to private equity, monopolists, or purely extractive owners.

The model should be tested sector by sector rather than assumed to fit every firm. It is most promising where the business has stable cash flow, a durable workforce, and a founder succession problem.

States should offer free or low-cost services to enterprises to help them evaluate and, where appropriate, convert into entities like this.

Private enterprise depends on public law, public infrastructure, educated workers, stable currency, courts, roads, energy systems, communications networks, basic research, and social trust.

This does not mean the public owns every firm or that private success is illegitimate.

It does mean that when public systems absorb major downside risk, provide large subsidies, create protected markets, or rescue failing institutions, the public should receive fair upside, enforceable conditions, or durable public benefits.

Possible forms include:

  • Equity warrants or revenue-sharing when public money rescues private firms.
  • Conditions on subsidies, procurement, and industrial policy.
  • Clawbacks for firms that take public support and then offshore, extract, or collapse obligations.
  • Public-interest licensing for publicly funded research where appropriate.
  • Community benefit agreements for major public investments.
  • Transparency around subsidies, bailouts, procurement, and lobbying.
  • Public reporting on the beneficiaries of public risk-bearing.
  • Automatic penalties for violating subsidy conditions.

The principle is not hostility to private profit.

The principle is symmetry: those who receive public risk-bearing should not keep all upside while shifting losses to workers, taxpayers, communities, or future generations.

A just political economy must also address finance.

Markets cannot be fair if the financial system repeatedly privatizes gains while socializing losses.

Reforms should aim to:

  • Reduce bailouts that rescue private upside without public accountability.
  • Support public, cooperative, or state-level banking options.
  • Expand patient capital for local enterprise.
  • Finance worker ownership transitions.
  • Provide capital for entrepreneurs without requiring extreme personal risk or dependence on concentrated private investors.
  • Discourage extractive leverage and speculative structures that destabilize communities.
  • Require public upside when public institutions absorb private risk.
  • Stress-test financial reforms against contagion, liquidity shocks, and political capture.

Finance should serve productive investment, broad ownership, and resilience, not merely asset inflation, extraction, or insider advantage.

A moral floor must be fiscally and macroeconomically durable. A promise that cannot survive recession, inflation, trade shocks, demographic change, or administrative weakness is not yet a real institution.

Every major floor, ownership, subsidy, or public-investment proposal should answer:

  • What is the funding base?
  • Is the funding cyclical or stable?
  • What happens during recession?
  • What happens during inflation?
  • What happens during a supply shock?
  • What happens during a trade shock?
  • What happens if interest rates rise?
  • What happens if productivity growth slows?
  • What happens if capital exits or firms relocate?
  • What is protected first if fiscal space tightens?
  • What automatic stabilizers expand during crisis?
  • What automatic brakes prevent runaway commitments?
  • How does the policy affect domestic competitiveness and comparative advantage?
  • Does the policy help workers move from stagnant sectors into more productive ones?
  • Does it create long-term resilience or merely short-term relief?

The goal is not austerity for its own sake. The goal is durable justice.

A society that promises a floor must be able to maintain it under stress.

Measurement is necessary, but measurement is not sovereignty.

Dashboards can discipline moral judgment, reveal blind spots, and make institutional performance visible. But dashboards are diagnostic instruments, not moral authorities.

Some rights are side constraints, not variables to be traded away for aggregate gains.

These include:

  • freedom of speech
  • freedom of association
  • freedom of conscience
  • due process
  • equal protection
  • political participation
  • appeal rights
  • protection from arbitrary punishment
  • protection from discrimination
  • the right to contest decisions that shape one’s life

A policy that raises income while increasing surveillance, suppressing dissent, humiliating recipients, or denying appeal rights has not automatically passed the justice test.

Public metrics must themselves be democratically contestable. What a society measures shapes what it notices, values, funds, and repairs.

Therefore, affected communities should have a role in defining, revising, and challenging the variables used to judge floor, power, status, and institutional capacity.

The model needs measurable tests so that “floor,” “power,” “status,” and “capacity” do not become slogans.

A floor dashboard could track:

  • Poverty and deep poverty.
  • Child poverty.
  • Housing burden and homelessness.
  • Medical bankruptcy and untreated illness.
  • Food insecurity.
  • Internet access.
  • Transportation access.
  • Educational attainment and literacy.
  • Household emergency savings.
  • Geographic access to jobs and services.
  • Energy burden and infrastructure resilience.

A power dashboard could track:

  • Labor market concentration.
  • Employer monopsony indicators.
  • Noncompete and anti-mobility restrictions.
  • Works council or worker voice coverage.
  • Rent burden and landlord concentration.
  • Household debt stress.
  • Market concentration by sector.
  • Platform dependency for small businesses and workers.
  • Political spending concentration.
  • Bailout, subsidy, and procurement concentration.
  • Administrative discretion and appeal outcomes.

A status dashboard could track:

  • Intergenerational mobility.
  • Wealth concentration and inherited advantage.
  • Discrimination in housing, credit, education, healthcare, and labor markets.
  • Civic participation gaps.
  • Criminal justice exclusion and reintegration outcomes.
  • Regional abandonment or persistent place-based disadvantage.
  • Public trust and perceived institutional fairness.
  • Measures of humiliation, exclusion, and institutional disrespect where these can be responsibly gathered.

An institutional capacity dashboard could track:

  • Administrative cost.
  • Processing time.
  • Error rates.
  • Appeal rates.
  • Appeal success rates.
  • Fraud and abuse.
  • Program complexity.
  • Public comprehension.
  • Tax collection efficiency.
  • Enforcement consistency.
  • Capture risk.
  • Conflict-of-interest violations.
  • Lobbying concentration.
  • Sunset and reauthorization outcomes.
  • Whether programs shrink, revise, or persist after failure.

These measurements should not replace moral judgment, but they can discipline it.

The floor, power, status, and capacity tests can conflict.

A policy might raise income but increase surveillance.

A subsidy might create jobs but entrench corporate dependence.

A regulation might reduce private domination but increase state discretion.

A worker-ownership rule might increase voice but reduce access to capital in some sectors.

A simple universal benefit might reduce stigma but create fiscal stress.

A local experiment might increase adaptation but weaken equal rights.

When guardrails conflict, the model should ask:

  • Is there a less dominating way to achieve the same floor gain?
  • Are affected people given voice, appeal, and exit?
  • Are the burdens transparent and fairly distributed?
  • Is the policy reversible if it fails?
  • Does it increase long-term institutional resilience or create new dependency?
  • Are we solving the problem at the lowest competent level while preserving universal rights?
  • Does the reform require discretion that cannot be safely bounded?
  • Are rights being treated as constraints or as variables to be traded away?
  • Is the reform fiscally durable under stress?
  • Is the institution capable of administering the promise it makes?

No institution should be judged by intentions alone. It should be judged by outcomes, side effects, capacity, incentives, and reversibility.

The philosophy and the policy experiments should be kept distinct.

The philosophy is: civilize luck, preserve agency, prevent domination, maintain plural institutions, protect equal standing, reward productive contribution, and limit both private and public domination.

The policy experiments are possible tools for achieving those aims.

A failed tool does not automatically refute the philosophy, and a morally attractive tool is not justified merely because it fits the philosophy.

Every proposal must show that it works better than available alternatives under real institutional conditions.

That means each reform should answer:

  • What specific floor, power, status, or capacity problem does it address?
  • Why is this tool better than simpler alternatives?
  • What new power does it create, and who might capture it?
  • What are the exit, appeal, and correction mechanisms?
  • What evidence would count as success or failure?
  • How can the reform be revised, shrunk, or ended if it fails?
  • What rights are non-negotiable?
  • What is the funding model?
  • What happens under fiscal, macroeconomic, or trade stress?

The model should begin with practical, testable reforms rather than total system replacement.

These are candidate experiments, not mandatory commitments. They should be adopted only where they satisfy the burden of proof and remain consistent with the four guardrails.

  • Universal child benefit.
  • Negative income tax or basic income floor.
  • Catastrophic health coverage.
  • Preventive basic healthcare.
  • Housing and transit guarantees where needed.
  • Universal high-speed internet.
  • Basic nutritional support.
  • Retraining pathways for displaced workers.
  • Affordable clean energy and resilient infrastructure.
  • Works councils.
  • Sectoral bargaining.
  • Whistleblower protection.
  • Portable benefits.
  • Due-process protections at work.
  • Employee representation in governance where feasible.
  • Open-book management norms where feasible.
  • Restrictions on coercive noncompete agreements and mobility traps.
  • Appeal rights against arbitrary institutional decisions.
  • Employee Ownership Trusts.
  • Worker cooperatives.
  • Cooperative finance.
  • Community Investment Trusts.
  • Public investment banks.
  • Anti-monopoly policy.
  • Founder-to-worker ownership transition incentives.
  • Tax neutrality between labor income and capital strategies.
  • Limits on subsidies for extractive leverage.
  • Voluntary templates and financing for ownership transitions.
  • Stronger antitrust enforcement.
  • Limits on monopoly and platform gatekeeping.
  • Restrictions on regulatory capture.
  • Public upside when public money absorbs private risk.
  • Anti-corruption rules around subsidies, bailouts, procurement, and lobbying.
  • Transparent reporting of public support and private beneficiaries.
  • Firewalls between regulated entities and review bodies.
  • Legible rules that minimize discretionary favoritism.
  • Floor, power, status, and capacity dashboards.
  • Fiscal sustainability bands.
  • Automatic stabilizers.
  • Automatic brakes for runaway commitments.
  • Independent review councils with anti-capture protections.
  • Public reporting on floor, power, status, and capacity outcomes.
  • Sunset clauses for experimental programs.
  • Stress tests for administrative complexity and state overreach.
  • Stress tests for recession, inflation, supply shocks, trade shocks, and interest-rate shocks.
  • Public audits of lobbying, procurement, subsidy concentration, and conflicts of interest.

A moral framework is not enough. The model must prove itself institution by institution.

No reform is justified merely because it sounds egalitarian, democratic, pro-market, anti-domination, pro-worker, pro-growth, or pro-liberty.

Each reform must show that it improves real outcomes without producing greater capture, coercion, dependency, complexity, fiscal fragility, state abuse, or institutional failure.

Each proposed reform should answer:

  • What problem does it solve?
  • Who benefits?
  • Who pays?
  • What incentives does it create?
  • What new powers does it create?
  • Who can capture it?
  • How does someone appeal abuse?
  • How does the policy end, shrink, or change if it fails?
  • What would count as success?
  • What would count as failure?
  • What rights are protected regardless of measured outcomes?
  • What happens under fiscal and macroeconomic stress?
  • Can the institution administer the policy competently?

The model should prefer reforms that are modular, reversible, evidence-generating, rights-preserving, administratively legible, and scalable only after demonstrated success.

Every major institution should be reviewed against six questions:

  1. Are the worst-off actually better protected?
  1. Is power becoming more concentrated or less?
  1. Are people treated with equal civic status?
  1. Is the system becoming simpler and more trustworthy, or more opaque and captured?
  1. Are gains and losses allocated fairly, or are private actors keeping upside while shifting downside onto workers, taxpayers, communities, or future generations?
  1. Is the institution competent, fundable, auditable, reversible, and resistant to capture?

Additional review questions should include:

  • Is the state gaining too much unchecked discretion?
  • Are private actors gaining too much unchecked discretion?
  • Are people able to contest decisions that affect them?
  • Are local institutions adapting within universal rights?
  • Are productive incentives preserved?
  • Are reforms strengthening agency or creating dependency?
  • Are rights being treated as side constraints?
  • Are dashboards being used as diagnostic tools rather than moral substitutes?
  • Are the fiscal commitments durable under stress?
  • Are the administrative demands realistic?
  • Are programs that fail actually revised, shrunk, or ended?

This model is not anti-market. It keeps markets where they generate discovery, innovation, coordination, competition, experimentation, and productive risk-taking.

It is not equal-outcome egalitarianism. It allows inequality when inequality is compatible with equal standing, raises or protects the floor, and does not become domination.

It is not state socialism. It explicitly worries about state domination and requires constitutional limits, due process, local adaptation, transparency, appeal rights, bounded discretion, and institutional checks.

It is not anti-entrepreneurship. It rewards founders, investors, judgment, risk, excellence, and productive coordination while rejecting the idea that success automatically justifies permanent unchecked command over others.

It is not a denial of responsibility. It treats responsibility as practical and forward-looking: prevention, repair, accountability, incentive design, and reintegration.

It is not a blueprint to impose all reforms everywhere. It is a framework for testing institutions against floor, power, status, and capacity outcomes.

It is not technocracy. Dashboards can inform judgment, but rights, dignity, voice, and democratic contestation remain central.

It is not localism without limits. Local communities may adapt, supplement, and exceed public guarantees, but they may not override universal rights or minimum floors.

It is not ownership uniformity. Broad ownership is a preferred direction, not a one-size-fits-all mandate.

The model can be summarized this way:

Build a society where no one’s life chances are hostage to brute luck, inherited caste, monopoly power, employer domination, landlord domination, creditor domination, platform dependency, political capture, or state overreach; where markets remain creative but are bounded by justice; where entrepreneurship is rewarded without becoming permanent rule over others; where public risk-bearing receives public accountability; where ownership, voice, opportunity, and exit power are broad enough for people to stand as equals; and where the institutions created to pursue justice are themselves simple, accountable, fundable, auditable, reversible, and resistant to capture.

The goal is not to abolish markets, erase difference, or centralize society under one institution.

The goal is to build a pluralist market democracy in which freedom is not merely the freedom of the already powerful, equality is not purchased at the cost of agency, and justice is not entrusted to institutions that cannot govern themselves.
