Finding What Is Worth Inventing

Two complementary methods for investigating future human behavior: Lead User Research and Behavioral Simulation.

Someone is coordinating three AI agents. Each has a separate context. Two make incompatible decisions. Before work can continue, the person reconstructs what happened and writes a shared log. Eventually they build a small tool to maintain it.

What did this person need? Who else is already encountering the same conditions? And what might happen if conflicts were marked directly on the artifact? The first questions concern behavior we can investigate. The last concerns behavior under a changed condition.

Eric von Hippel's Lead User Method gives us a way to investigate such questions. Instead of asking a representative customer what they want tomorrow, we look for people already encountering conditions that others may experience later. Then we learn from what they have done.

I've made two open-source research skills. Lead User Research investigates emerging needs using real human evidence. Behavioral Simulation explores what people might do under changed conditions. Use them in both directions. When real evidence is scarce, simulation can help you find overlooked circumstances, search for people who may experience an emerging need, and decide which observation would be most informative. When evidence becomes available, it can ground stronger simulations. You do not need a proposed product to start. The AI coordination example throughout is fictional; its purpose is to demonstrate the method, not establish a market opportunity.

1. Frame the inquiry2. Map the frontier3. Study real behavior4. Challenge and freeze evidence5. Transfer the need6. Simulate changed conditions7. Learn from the results

1. Frame the inquiry

Start with a decision, not a product. “Should we investigate a new way to coordinate agentic work?” is more useful than “Would people buy my AI dashboard?” Specify the target market, learning objective, innovation altitude (workflow, product category or system), and any hypotheses. Missing low-risk fields can be proposed as provisional; consequential scope must not be invented.

2. Map the frontier

Establish the trend first. What is changing, in what direction, and what observable condition puts one person ahead of another? A lead user is not a permanent type of person. Qualification concerns a particular episode, relative to a particular trend.

Consider a hypothetical hierarchy of advancement: occasional AI use → delegated tasks → coordination of several agents across projects → sustained large-scale agent operations. The frontier is not necessarily the most commercially relevant position. An advanced user may have little to gain from a particular improvement; a mainstream user may urgently need one.

Follow knowledge, not popularity

Search public need–solution pairs: problems, attempts, failures, workarounds, modifications and outcomes. Follow citations, forks, specialist communities, experts and direct referrals toward people who are further ahead. Pyramiding is a way to discover candidates, not a ranking of people's worth.

For a study, a useful additional view is a frontier map: plot each evidenced episode relative to its trend, annotate its advancement and benefit signals, connect referral and lineage relationships, mark independent branches and analogs, and flag unanswered questions. This is a proposed visualization of existing research records, not a formal ranking or a currently implemented scoring feature.

Look outside the category

Find domains where the same functional problem appears under more extreme conditions: air traffic control for coordination, intensive care for handoffs, industrial control rooms for oversight, or site reliability engineering for distributed failure detection. These are hypotheses about useful analogs, not established similarities.

Scan changes in technology, cost, regulation, science and infrastructure too. A new capability can make new behavior possible before anyone has invented a workaround. Map capability change → feasible behavior → implications for solutions → relevance to evidenced need. Treat these as context, not human evidence. Search volume, stars, fame, technical sophistication and prototype polish likewise guide discovery; they don't establish demand.

Expand the search when evidence is scarce

Suppose public research turns up eight advanced practitioners. Their traces reveal costly coordination work, but private teams and less visible workflows remain unobserved. Instead of asking a model to invent ninety-two additional interviewees, investigate the missing circumstances. Vary team size, technical expertise, access to shared context, incentives and infrastructure. Ask which needs might emerge under each condition, which rival explanations might account for the original traces, and where a real example could be found.

Use the resulting hypotheses to broaden pyramiding, search for independent branches and examine attribute-specific analogs. A model may suggest a kind of person or a query; it must not invent a real candidate, source or referral. If search cannot locate sufficient evidence, the gap remains open.

Explore how the frontier might move

A trend is not just a list of advanced people. It is a changing distribution of conditions. What happens when access becomes cheaper, expertise becomes less necessary, or organizations learn from each other? Which currently exceptional constraints disappear? What new constraints might arise? Multi-agent dynamics is useful when diffusion and feedback matter, while population prediction requires an independently defensible real population frame.

3. Study real behavior

Find bounded episodes, not just feature requests. Prefer behavioral artifacts and traces, then first-person accounts and independent observations. A person maintaining a costly workaround may tell us more than an influential person asking for a feature.

Record the initiating condition, prior approach, actual steps, expected progress, outcome, recurrence and abandonment where observable. Keep observed behavior, stated purpose, inferred purpose and unknowns distinct. A detailed first-person account or event-log reconstruction can support a trace; neither automatically proves motive or causality.

When a decision-critical fact cannot be observed from traces, ask targeted questions or observe people directly. If it can be observed, examine the available traces first. If it can safely remain unknown, record why. AI-only research is desk research, even in Full mode. Direct fieldwork can enrich it; a full classical Lead User project also involves real lead users and experts in collaborative concept development.

4. Challenge and freeze the evidence

Write down a prediction and the strongest plausible refuter before broad discovery. Consider rival explanations. A hypothesis can survive current tests; it is not confirmed forever.

Predicted positive

Teams using multiple agents struggle to coordinate.

Exposure without outcome

Other multi-agent teams do not struggle.

Outcome without exposure

Teams without agents have the same struggle.

Abandoned solution

A coordination workaround is abandoned or becomes unnecessary.

These contrastive cases test explanations, not prevalence. Check lineage so a widely copied workaround is not counted as independent corroboration. Ask what public search systematically misses: private enterprise, offline practitioners, non-English communities and tacit procedural innovations. Record platform context, too. An issue tracker and a support queue expose different behaviors.

Freeze the evidence before preferring a product concept. A consequential insufficiency sends the agent back to discovery or evidence; post-freeze changes are recorded and re-evaluated. AI extraction and coding that materially influence evidence need task-specific sampled validation. A deterministic validator checks structure and gates, not whether an interpretation is true. Human review, structural validation and interpretive stability remain separate.

5. Transfer the need, not the workaround

Only after the evidence is frozen, interpret across episodes. The shared log is a mechanism. A possible need is to preserve decisions and unresolved questions across agent handoffs without reconstructing context. A transferable principle might be to make the current state and its provenance visible. These are different levels of claim.

Use Christensen's Jobs to Be Done as a limited lens on circumstance, struggle, desired progress and compensating behavior. Do not invent missing motives to complete the story. Preserve contradictions and outliers.

Before shaping, examine differences in expertise, cost tolerance, maintenance, safety, regulation and workflow. Assess propagation separately: will the underlying need spread as the trend advances? Evidence of one high-benefit need doesn't tell us how common it will become. Keep observations, inferred needs, trend projections, market hypotheses and product hypotheses distinct.

6. Simulate changed conditions

Once a real need is supported, a different question may arise. What happens if an agent flags conflicts on the artifact itself? Do people investigate sooner, ignore warnings, or over-trust the system? Lead User Research cannot establish these counterfactual outcomes. Behavioral Simulation is a sibling method for exploring them.

Plausibility space

Explore possible behaviors, edge cases and mechanisms. Do not predict prevalence.

Individual proxy

Ground a proxy in a person's own interviews, traces and artifacts.

Population prediction

Estimate a distribution for a defined population with defensible real data.

Multi-agent dynamics

Explore interaction, memory, rules, feedback and trajectories over time.

Turn a finding into a counterfactual

Suppose real evidence supported the need to locate conflicts without reconstructing the context of several agents. Specify a changed condition: a shared artifact marks each suspected conflict and links to the supporting evidence. The simulation asks what a defined person or population might do differently under that condition.

Define population, baseline, intervention, outcome and horizon first. Register observations, nonhuman context and assumptions separately. Specify model/version, seeds, metrics and counterfactual distance. Validate against genuinely held-out outcomes at the correct unit; individual accuracy is not distributional alignment. For categorical distributions, total variation distance is half the sum of absolute differences in probabilities. Calibration tests whether predicted error is useful on new questions, not whether the model sounds confident. Examine subgroup errors, leakage, model sensitivity, drift and freshness.

Even a validated simulation remains a simulated estimate. Ten thousand synthetic agents do not constitute a sample of ten thousand real people. Simulation cannot backfill LU1/LU2, prevalence, need importance, observed behavior, willingness to pay or market economics. Use it to expose failure modes, compare probes and prioritize real-world tests; it can carry more decision weight only within its demonstrated scope.

Measure simulated responses without pretending they are observations

Three additional papers give us techniques for using simulations more carefully. Maier and colleagues' Semantic Similarity Rating (SSR) elicits natural-language responses, compares their embeddings with predeclared verbal anchors, and produces a distribution over ordinal ratings. Use it when the question actually concerns a stated attitude, not when you need to establish observed behavior. The published concept-ranking results are not individual purchase accuracy.

Krsteski and colleagues' Valid Survey Simulations with Limited Human Data demonstrates a different operation: correct a population mean with real paired responses. In a suitable sampling design, preserve some human outcomes for correction rather than spending the entire budget on fine-tuning. The human and unlabeled real covariate samples must represent the same target distribution; the predictor must be frozen independently. A convenience group of eight lead users cannot support this calculation.

Bisbee and colleagues' Synthetic Replacements for Human Survey Data? shows why matching averages is insufficient: simulated variance and relationships among variables can differ materially from human data. Examine dispersion, distribution, subgroup error, conditional relationships and model/prompt/time sensitivity. A model that matches a mean can still be wrong about who needs what.

These are not new simulation modes. SSR measures text; rectification estimates a population parameter; fidelity checks test the reliability of estimates. None establishes a lead user's advancement or benefit. The shared implementation is in review: Behavioral Simulation revision.

What simulation might surface

Useful intervention

A person investigates the marked conflict sooner because the relevant context is accessible.

Over-trust

A person accepts a false warning and interrupts work that was progressing correctly.

Missed problem

A person assumes unmarked areas are safe and overlooks a consequential error.

Adaptation

A person learns to inspect certain warnings and ignore others, changing how the system is used over time.

These are hypothetical mechanisms, not outputs of a completed simulation or observations of real people.

At L0, possibilities like these expand what we consider and help plan a test. To estimate actual response distributions or effect sizes, obtain relevant human data, hold out outcomes, measure error against a meaningful baseline and calibrate for the intended decision. A novel interface may be far outside the data used to validate a simulator; say so.

7. Learn from the results

Return to the decision at the start. Recommend act, test, hold or reject (Scout: stop, investigate or escalate). The brief should state why, the decisive evidence, what remains uncertain, the action now and what would change the decision. A negative result is a valid result.

Return to reality

Use the possible behaviors from simulation to prioritize a real-world test. Compare the existing workflow with conflict annotations in a controlled task. Measure detection, correction time, missed conflicts, unnecessary interventions and trust errors. Compare what actually happens with the model's estimates. If real people behave differently, preserve the discrepancy and revise the model or the research question. Do not silently reinterpret the observation to fit the simulation.

A research loop can begin on either side: a real episode can suggest a simulation, and a plausible scenario can suggest a search for real cases. The loop ends when we have learned enough for the decision, not when every tool has been used.

Keep the work inspectable

For file-capable agents, initialize a new study workspace and persist structured evidence, hypotheses, observability, analysis, lineage, decisions and change logs. Reopen state each phase; use the phase controller to find the smallest valid next move. Validate the workspace and render the canonical Decision Brief. PDF and HTML are derived views. In ordinary chat, save a cumulative state packet at each phase; do not pretend conversational memory is persistent state.

The tools provide safeguards and an implementation of a methodology, not proof that the resulting study is scientifically valid. Review the evidence yourself. A model's checklist is not independent review. The reference study is explicitly synthetic; its validator-ready records demonstrate the workflow rather than establish empirical findings.

We are trying to learn about behavior we can observe today and behavior we cannot yet observe. One method looks outward to real people at the frontier of change. The other constructs conditions and examines possible responses. Keeping them distinct lets us use them together.

The research behind the method

The eight papers inform different parts of the method: Social Simulacra (possible behaviors); Generative Agents (memory, interaction and diffusion); Generative Agent Simulations of 1,000 People (person-specific grounding); Finetuning LLMs for Human Behavior Prediction (population distributions and held-out studies); On the Opportunities and Risks of Foundation Models (inherited bias); SSR (ordinal measurement); Valid Survey Simulations (correction); and Synthetic Replacements (fidelity safeguards). Simile's public methodology adds a design reference for empirical error prediction and calibration. The combined Lead User method has not yet been empirically validated.
