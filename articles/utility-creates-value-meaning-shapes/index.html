Utility Creates Value | Meaning Shapes Desire

The Interplay of Product & Brand

The first time a product does something you could not do before, part of its value is the discovery itself.

You touch it. Something happens. You try again. What seemed impossible becomes available, then understandable, then ordinary.

Interaction design gives that discovery a sequence: what happens first, what follows, how one action changes what becomes possible next. At its best, it produces wonder—attention sharpened by what the thing does and what it reveals through use.

But products do not remain new.

The feature spreads. Competitors learn. What once looked miraculous becomes expected. The capability may still be useful, even essential, but some of its wonder disappears through familiarity.

Something else can move in the opposite direction.

A company makes a choice, then another. What it emphasizes. What it refuses. What it rewards. The way the product behaves when something goes wrong. The things it makes easy and the things it makes deliberately hard.

One choice means little. Repeated choices begin to form a pattern.

Brand organizes that continuity.

Over time, the pattern can acquire meaning: what the product suggests, what it stands for, what kind of person might choose it, what kind of world it seems to belong to, what possibilities it lets people imagine for themselves.

Novelty fades.

Meaning can accumulate.

Where functional differences narrow, meaning becomes another dimension of competition. Products may solve roughly the same problem while accumulating different aesthetics and associations, different values and identities, different ideas about the life surrounding the product.

Those differences do not have to be unique, deeply held, or consciously articulated by every buyer to matter. Much of what people carry about brands is weak, partial, situational, implicit.

Sometimes simple recognition is enough.

And yet those associations can outlast the particular attributes people value at any one moment. What counts as performance, quality, or satisfaction changes as technologies and expectations change. A durable brand gives a company room to change without becoming unrecognizable.

But meaning is not applied afterward.

Not a wrapper around utility. Not an interpretive layer laid over it.

It is produced, in part, by the thing itself.

A product that makes an irreversible action slightly harder can turn friction into evidence of care. The same friction somewhere else can feel like indifference. A recommendation can feel helpful or self-serving. A restriction can feel principled or arbitrary.

The behavior is the evidence.

What the product does and how it feels become part of what it means.

With familiarity, surprise can recede even as meaning grows. What first delighted you can become something quieter: expected, trusted, recognizable.

A pattern.

Brand carries that pattern from one encounter to the next. Distinctiveness tells people whose pattern it is.

That distinction matters. A behavior, sound, shape, phrase, color, interaction, or disposition can become recognizable without becoming recognizable as yours. Distinctive assets attach the accumulated pattern to the company that created it.

But companies do not determine meaning alone.

People interpret the pattern, respond to it, and sometimes make it their own.

Surprise makes the pattern noticeable. Humor or seriousness gives it character. Craft makes it feel intentional; restraint keeps it coherent. Generosity, precision, irreverence, and other qualities give it a disposition.

Repeated long enough, these stop feeling decorative.

They become evidence.

At their deepest, the meanings that gather around products can reach past preference toward elemental human desires: to love and be loved, to belong and be seen, to desire and become, to hold on and let go.

Recognition. Longing. Aspiration. Delight. Nostalgia. Trust. Hope. Awe.

The feelings that can gather around a thing once it means more than what it does.

Not every successful brand needs to reach this depth. Most buyers will never hold an elaborate theory of what a brand stands for. Meaning can remain shallow, fragmentary, and situational and still affect perception. Sometimes what matters is simply that something feels familiar, appropriate, easy to recognize when a choice appears.

Meaning can also turn against the person experiencing it.

Belonging into exclusion. Aspiration into status anxiety. Longing into dependence.

The aim should not be to manufacture a wound the product can then promise to heal, but to make genuine value easier to recognize and new possibilities easier to imagine.

And sometimes people begin to enact the pattern themselves—through habits, rituals, language, taste, affiliation.

Brand becoming culture.

Interaction design shapes what value people can discover.

Brand shapes what those discoveries come to mean.

Meaning shapes desire.

But desire is not purchase.

A brand still has to enter the choice.

Someone gets hungry. Needs toothpaste. Wants something cold on a hot afternoon. Opens an app looking for a place to stay. The category appears before the brand does.

Which names arrive with it?

Mental availability makes a brand easier to retrieve when the relevant situation occurs. Physical availability makes it easier to find and buy once it does.

No profound relationship is required. A person may encounter a familiar cue at the right moment, remember a brand, find it readily available, and choose it.

Meaning can shape desire.

Mental availability helps determine what enters consideration.

Physical availability determines what can actually be chosen.

These are different jobs.

What a product makes possible gives it utility. What a company repeatedly makes, emphasizes, refuses, rewards, and chooses forms a pattern. What people make of that pattern gives it meaning.

Meaning shapes desire.

Branding organizes the choices that matter most in how a company relates to people, making the pattern they form coherent, recognizable, and legible.

Fit is where what people value meets what the company can credibly deliver.

Positioning chooses which aspects of that value, which associations, and which buying situations to emphasize.

Branding makes the company recognizable and those associations easier to retrieve across what it makes, says, and does.

Distinctiveness tells people whose pattern it is.

None of this removes the need to reach people beyond the most committed customers. Brands grow by reaching more buyers—including light buyers—appearing in more relevant buying situations, and becoming easier to remember and easier to buy.

Strong meaning can improve the quality of a relationship.

It does not substitute for availability.

Two questions, then:

Why might someone want this?

Will this brand come to mind and be available when that wanting becomes a choice?

A strong brand has to contend with both.

The business model provides one of the clearest tests of the larger idea, because incentives eventually become behavior people can experience.

Pricing. Advertising. Recommendations. Rankings. Fees. Defaults. The rules governing all of them.

These may begin as economic decisions behind the product. But once people can feel their effects inside the experience, they become evidence about whose interests the company serves.

A business model is not merely machinery behind the product.

Its consequences enter the pattern.

Advertising makes the point especially clearly.

It has become one of the internet’s most durable consumer business models because it can keep utility broadly accessible while monetizing commercial relevance. In many consumer products, that can produce stronger economics than asking every user to subscribe.

AI companies do not have to invent this machinery from scratch. They inherit decades of learning about marketplaces, auctions, targeting, ranking, and the tradeoffs between paid and organic content.

But inheritance is not destiny.

The strongest version does not simply give the highest bidder access to attention.

Paid and unpaid options can participate in the same decision system, with commercial value weighed alongside usefulness and relevance to the person. A Vickrey–Clarke–Groves-style mechanism offers one model if the allocation function incorporates user value alongside advertiser value: payment can influence which commercial option wins without making payment the only thing the system optimizes.

The less useful the commercial option, the less compelling its claim on the interaction should become.

And at some point, payment should not be enough.

For products people rely on for judgment, the scarce resource may be trust.

A feed can compare a paid item with the organic value of whatever would otherwise appear. An AI assistant has a harder problem. A commercial intervention can alter not only what someone sees, but what they believe the system is trying to do for them.

That changes the object being optimized.

Commercial influence can add utility rather than merely interrupt it. A recommendation, offer, or advertisement can help someone discover a better option, make a better decision, or receive something useful at the right moment.

The problem begins when what generates revenue starts working against what the person came to the product for.

One system repeatedly favors what is useful and relevant, even when someone else would pay for greater influence.

Another repeatedly lets payment override relevance.

People may never formulate the distinction explicitly.

They still experience it.

Behavior becomes expectation. Expectation becomes part of the relationship. The relationship becomes evidence about the company.

Trust can be preserved, spent, or compounded.

So the question is not simply how much revenue an interaction can bear. It is how much commercial influence the relationship can bear before the product begins to mean something different.

That makes the design problem a long-term one.

A product can remain functionally excellent while gradually teaching people to wonder whether its rankings, recommendations, or answers serve them or whoever paid.

Nothing has to break.

The doubt is enough.

Maximizing immediate revenue can therefore weaken the confidence that sustains continued use and the quality of the relationship, even while growth still depends on reaching new and light users.

The system has more than one thing to protect.

Revenue. Relevance. Utility. Trust.

And not as independent metrics.

As forces acting on the same relationship.

That principle should shape the marketplace itself. Commercial influence should earn its place through usefulness. The further an advertiser asks the product to depart from what would otherwise best serve the person, the harder that influence should become to justify.

Beyond some point, the system should refuse the trade.

Then there is the format itself.

Chat, voice, agents, and other AI interfaces are not feeds with a new coat of paint. The commercial unit that belongs inside them may not yet exist.

An ad in a conversational system may need to behave less like an inserted message and more like a useful participant in the interaction. In voice, where there may be no obvious visual border between answer and promotion, the problem becomes harder still.

What is the advertisement?

Where does the product end and the commercial influence begin?

How does the person know?

AI advertising is therefore not merely a media-placement problem. It is a product-design problem: how to create commercial formats that add value inside the interaction while preserving the person’s understanding of whose interests the system serves.

If that can be done, advertising need not always be something removed from the best version of a product.

In some consumer contexts, commercially supported recommendations may add enough value that even highly engaged or high-value users benefit from them. Removing advertising entirely from premium experiences may discard opportunities to invent forms of commercial participation that are genuinely useful.

Other contexts—focused work, coding, situations requiring especially high confidence—may call for different rules.

The larger principle remains.

A business model becomes part of the product when people experience its consequences.

Its incentives become behavior.

Its behavior becomes a pattern.

People make meaning from that pattern.

Meaning shapes desire.

But meaning is only one part of how brands grow.

People must encounter the brand. Recognize it. Retrieve it when the situation calls for it. Be able to choose it.

Utility creates value.

Experience creates evidence.

Repetition creates a pattern.

Distinctiveness tells people whose pattern it is.

Meaning shapes desire.

Mental availability helps the brand enter the choice.

Physical availability makes the choice possible.

The strongest brands do not have to choose between meaning and availability.

They become easier to recognize, easier to remember, easier to buy—and, encounter after encounter, clearer in what they have come to mean.

  1. Start with utility. What becomes possible, etc. because this exists?
  2. Design the discovery. Shape how people encounter that value: what happens first, what follows, what they learn through use.
  3. Choose what to reinforce. Decide which value, associations, and buying situations matter most. Don’t force profundity; meaning can be simple and situational.
  4. Build and test the pattern. Make, emphasize, reward, refuse, and repeat deliberately. Ask: What would someone infer from seeing this choice again and again—and what evidence would show that they actually do?
  5. Make it attributable. Use distinctive cues so people know whose pattern they are experiencing.
  6. Enter the choice. Build memory around relevant situations, then remove barriers to finding, accessing, and buying the product.
  7. Treat economics as experience. Audit pricing, ranking, ads, defaults, and incentives for what they teach people about whose interests you serve.
  8. Protect trust. Commercial influence must earn its place through usefulness. Refuse trades that change what the product means.

Final test:

Does it create value?

What does the experience teach?

What pattern does it reinforce?

What evidence shows people actually learn that pattern?

Will they recognize us, remember us, and be able to choose us?
