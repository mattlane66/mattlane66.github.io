01 · Where we started

Powerful — once you knew what to search for.

Sample

A short audio clip — a drum loop, vocal phrase, bass line.

DAW

Where you actually produce — Ableton Live, Logic, Studio One.

The business

A subscription to the catalog. Value depended on what you could do with it.

What I did: mapped the path creators actually took — search, audition, drag sounds into the DAW one by one — and the point where they gave up without an idea.
02 · The real problem

Finding sounds is not the same as making progress.

Creator research

Kept surfacing the same blank-canvas friction at the start of a session.

Behavioral signals

Search and browse alone weren't pulling people into repeat creation.

Business pressure

Marketplace subscribers were declining coming out of COVID.

What I did: put three independent signals together into one diagnosis — the gap was assembly, not discovery.
03 · The strategic reframe

Move the product one step later.

Customer bet

Less friction at the start creates faster, repeatable progress.

Business bet

Repeatable progress makes the subscription habit-forming.

Product principle

Augment the start. Don't replace the studio.

What I did: proposed this shift to leadership and framed the problem it had to solve.
04 · The decision that shaped everything

How much of the creative workflow should Splice own?

The assumption underneath: creators finish tracks in their own DAW. The boundary, the requirements and the success metrics all rest on it; if it were wrong, they would be wrong together. That is why export was non-negotiable.
What I did: made the boundary call — the guided song starter over better discovery and a full composition tool. Appendix A shows the Must-haves each option had to pass.
05 · What we actually built

One loop, one exit.

What I did: set the hard edges — limited editing, export-first, and no composition environment, social layer or unconstrained generation. Each cut protected scope, creator trust or product identity.
06 · What the AI had to accomplish

I defined what “good output” had to mean for the product.

The architecture belonged to ML / research. My job was to define the product requirements the AI had to satisfy — the bar every Stack was judged against.

Must-have

Worth continuing

The first result had to feel coherent enough to build from.

Must-have

Fast to steer

A creator had to be able to repair an idea without starting over.

Must-have

Catalog-native

Every layer had to remain attributable, acquirable and usable.

Must-have

Workflow-fit

The result had to move cleanly back into the creator's existing production environment.

Each is a Must-have in the decision ledger (Appendix A). Section 08 is what happened when output cleared the surface checks but missed the first one.

What I did: defined the product requirements and evaluation bar. ML / research owned model design and implementation.
07 · How we sequenced the build

We ordered the work by uncertainty, not by feature.

What I did: wrote the slice plan and ordered it by risk — which is why the failure in the next section showed up early enough to act on.
08 · The thing we got wrong

The loop didn't end. That was the real failure.

Known risk

My slice plan asked it first: “Can the first result feel coherent enough to keep?”

My bet

That stronger output quality would answer it. I expected generation quality to be the hard part.

What was missing

A check for the thing that actually mattered. Surface compatibility was not the same as “worth continuing from.”

This is the moment the project changed — and the part I'd most want to dig into with you.
09 · What that learning changed

We replaced a loop that restarted with one that converges.

What I did: recentered the team on the creator's workflow, not the model's output — capping the layers, keeping editing simple, protecting the path back to the DAW, and measuring saves and exports instead of generation volume.
10 · What happened

Adoption beat our own launch benchmark.

Stacks (the song assistant)80%
Other Splice launches≈30%

Internal metric: 60-day adoption, against Splice's benchmark for other launches.

60%

subscription growth in under a year — roughly 500K → 800K subscribers, after a post‑COVID decline.

The claim: the broader creator-tool strategy this product was part of helped drive that growth.
Not the claim: that this product caused it alone.
Context: subscribers had been falling before this, so a turnaround says more than growth alone — though other things changed in that period too.
What I did: defined success as saves and exports rather than generation volume, with our own launch benchmark as the bar.
11 · What the wedge enabled

Same wedge. More doors into it.

Mine: the earlier strategy, the boundary, MVP framing and shaping. Not mine: the later surfaces the teams after me built.

The strategic confirmation: the wedge was right, and the next job was distribution and workflow depth.
12 · What changed how I work

Ambitious outcomes needed unusually narrow scope.

01

Quality is necessary, not sufficient.

People adopt systems they can understand, steer and recover from. I now treat control as a first-order requirement, and test it before investing in generation breadth.

02

The right wedge is smaller than you expect.

Own the step where friction is highest and value is immediate — not the whole adjacent market.

03

Say what you're not building, early.

Defining the cuts up front is what let ambition stay high while the build stayed shippable.
